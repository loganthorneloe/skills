percentages — How do I turn a percentage into a count?

assumed

Multiply the group size by the percentage divided by 100; divide a count by its group size to recover a proportion.

      joint_counts — How many flagged messages come from each actual class?

      supported

      For each class, multiply its population count by its within-class flag rate. In a constructed 10,000-message population: 100 spam × 0.9 = 90 true flags; 9,900 legitimate × 0.1 = 990 false flags. These are expected counts under the stipulated rates.

      update_rule — What general rule reverses the direction of the given probabilities?

      supported

      Let p be the starting spam fraction, a the fraction of spam showing the evidence, and b the fraction of legitimate messages showing it. The spam fraction after observing that evidence is ap / (ap + b(1-p)), provided the denominator is positive. Numerator counts spam with evidence; denominator counts every source of that evidence. This is Bayes’ rule for two classes.

      base_rate_change — How does a different spam base rate change that answer?

      supported

      With fixed flag rates and spam fraction p, the spam fraction among flags is 0.9p / (0.9p + 0.1(1-p)). Illustrative values: p=0.1% gives about 0.89%; 1% gives 8.33%; 10% gives 50%; 50% gives 90%. Changing population composition alone changes the fraction of flags that are correct.

      word_clue — How can a word act like a flag?

      supported

      Use word presence as evidence: a is its frequency among spam, b its frequency among legitimate messages. Apply the same update rule. When 0<p<1 and a>b, observing the word increases the spam fraction; equal positive a and b leave it unchanged. These follow by comparing ap/(ap+b(1-p)) with p.

      training — Where do the word frequencies and priors come from?

      supported

      independence — Why can naive Bayes multiply separate word-clue probabilities?

      supported

      It assumes that once the class is fixed, learning the other feature values does not change the probability of a feature value. This conditional independence assumption lets the within-class probability of the observed features become a product. It is not an assertion that words in language are independent.

      word_scores — How does naive Bayes combine several clues with a base rate?

      supported

      For each class, multiply its prior by its within-class feature probabilities. Divide a class score by the sum of both scores to obtain its model probability. In a presence/absence word model, every chosen word feature contributes its presence probability if present, or one minus that probability if absent.

      probability_limit — Why might a naive Bayes score fail to describe real-world confidence?

      supported

      Classifying usefully does not ensure reliable probabilities. Calibration compares predicted probabilities with observed class fractions among similarly scored messages. The 8.33% result is exact for the stipulated population rates; an estimated word-model probability needs empirical checking and does not guarantee an individual outcome.

      target — Why is a flag not almost certainly spam, and how do base rates and words determine the answer?

      supported

      The flag rates describe separate actual classes. At 1% spam, far more legitimate messages are available to make false flags, yielding only about 8.33% spam among flags. A different base rate changes the weighted contributions. Words provide evidence by the same conditioning rule; naive Bayes estimates word-feature rates within each class and combines them under conditional independence. Its model probabilities require empirical validation.
