Node answers and evidence

percent — How do percentages describe a chosen group?

assumed

A percentage is a count out of every hundred members of the group named in the statement. Multiplying a group size by its percentage as a decimal gives the corresponding count.

    counts — How many flags arise from each kind of mail?

    supported

    In a constructed population of 10,000 messages, 1% gives 100 spam and 9,900 legitimate messages. The rates yield 100 × 0.90 = 90 spam flags and 9,900 × 0.10 = 990 legitimate flags. These are model-expected counts, not a measured inbox.

    posterior — What fraction of flags really comes from spam?

    supported

    Among 90 + 990 = 1,080 flagged messages, 90 are spam: 90/1,080 = 1/12, or about 8.33%. The denominator is all flags. Most flags come from legitimate mail because there are 99 times as many legitimate messages initially. A randomly selected flagged message therefore has an 8.33% spam chance under the given rates; the flag raises the starting 1% chance but does not make spam likely.

    basechange — How does changing the starting spam share change the answer?

    supported

    Let p be the initial spam share written as a decimal. Holding the 90% detection and 10% false-positive rates fixed, the spam share among flags is (0.90 × p)/[(0.90 × p) + (0.10 × (1 − p))]. At starting shares 0.1%, 1%, 10%, and 50%, the answers are about 0.893%, 8.33%, 50%, and 90%. As the spam population grows, spam contributes more of the flags relative to legitimate mail. At 0% and 100% starting spam the answers are respectively 0% and 100%. These comparisons assume the within-class flagging rates remain unchanged.

    words — How can a word serve as evidence about spam?

    supported

    A feature is a measured property, such as whether the word “offer” occurs. Its likelihood within spam is the fraction of spam messages containing it; compare that with its fraction within legitimate mail. Treat its presence as the flag and use the same calculation. Hypothetically, if its frequencies were 90% and 10%, observing it at a 1% spam base rate would again give 8.33%. These invented word frequencies illustrate the rule; no actual word statistics are claimed.

    learn — Where do the word probabilities and prior come from?

    supported

    Labeled examples identify which messages are spam or legitimate. Class counts estimate the prior. For a presence feature, count messages containing the word within each class; for a word-count model, count word occurrences against total word occurrences in that class. Small added counts, called smoothing, prevent an unseen training word from forcing a zero estimate. Bernoulli models use presence and absence; multinomial models use occurrence counts.

    joint — How is the chance of two clues together found before assuming independence?

    supported

    Within spam, first count the fraction with clue one. Among that subset, count the fraction also having clue two. Multiply those fractions to obtain the spam share having both clues. Repeat within legitimate mail. The second fraction can differ from its frequency in the whole class.

    independence — When may individual word-clue probabilities be multiplied?

    supported

    Naive Bayes assumes that, after fixing spam or legitimate, learning the other feature values does not change a feature’s chance. It then replaces the second clue’s chance given both class and first clue with its chance given class alone, and multiplies. Real words may co-occur, so this simplification can count related evidence too strongly.

    scores — How do the prior and several word clues produce a classification?

    supported

    For each class, multiply its prior by the modeled chance of the complete observed features within that class. Divide the spam score by the sum of both scores to normalize; compare scores to choose the more likely class. In an explicitly hypothetical two-feature presence model, if each clue occurs in 90% of spam and 10% of legitimate mail and the clues are independent within each class, observing both gives scores 0.01 × 0.90 × 0.90 = 0.0081 and 0.99 × 0.10 × 0.10 = 0.0099, yielding 45% spam. If the clues always occur together, the second adds no information and the correct answer stays 8.33%. Bernoulli models also use absent features; a full message score is not generally a product over present words alone.

    limits — Does the classifier’s numerical probability guarantee real-world reliability?

    supported

    target — Why is a flag not almost certainly spam at a 1% base rate, how does the base rate change this, and how do words work as clues?

    supported

    At 1% spam prevalence, 90 real spam flags are outnumbered by 990 legitimate flags per 10,000 modeled messages, so only 8.33% of flags are spam. Detection among spam and spam among flags use different denominators. A larger spam base rate raises the latter share when the flagging rates stay fixed. Words work as evidence through their within-class frequencies; naive Bayes combines their modeled likelihoods with the class prior using conditional independence. Its normalized score need not be an empirically reliable confidence.

    Dependency reasons

    percent → conditional

    why
    Restricting a group is meaningful only once the denominator of its percentage is understood.

    percent → prior

    why
    A starting class share is a percentage of the entire incoming population.

    conditional → rates

    why
    Distinguishing the rates requires recognizing which condition selects the denominator.

    prior → counts

    why
    The starting shares determine how many spam and legitimate cases can generate flags.

    rates → counts

    why
    Each class count must be multiplied by its own within-class flagging rate.

    counts → total

    why
    The total flagged count is formed from the two calculated class contributions.

    total → posterior

    why
