## Transfer probe: baseline differences and randomization versus representativeness

**From A alone:** If app users were already better prepared, a higher later average may reflect that preparation rather than the app. Compare the preparation groups separately or reweight them to a common mix; this works causally only if the relevant selection factors were measured, both comparison groups exist, and use matches the specified intervention. Random assignment prevents preparation from systematically determining assignment across repetitions, but a particular randomized allocation can still have baseline imbalance by chance. Keep assigned groups intact and report uncertainty. A study in laptop-equipped Maine schools tests that package there; it cannot guarantee results in other settings. The distinction between within-study assignment and population selection is inferable from these limits, but A does not explicitly name or explain random sampling. Grounding: §§2–6.

**From B alone:** A baseline advantage among voluntary users is a possible common cause of use and exam scores. Equal prior scores alone do not eliminate unmeasured motivation. A randomized assignment can still yield unequal baseline averages by chance; it gives comparability in expectation, not identical groups in each realized study. Do not throw away assigned nonusers to improve the comparison. Separately, randomly assigning recruited students does not make recruitment representative of all students. Thus a sound trial among a selected school population may establish an effect there without establishing the same effect in different ages, subjects, access conditions, or app versions. Grounding: §§2–3, §5’s explicit assignment/sampling distinction, and §6’s observational assumptions.
