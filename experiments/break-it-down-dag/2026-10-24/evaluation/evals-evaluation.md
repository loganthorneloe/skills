## Transfer probe: a test set misses a new failure type

**From A alone:** A high score on the old cases does not establish performance on the omitted situation. For example, if a new order-lookup timeout makes the assistant claim a refund was issued, preserve the observed response and trace, classify the false-action claim as critical, and add the timeout scenario with an explicit expected behavior. Inspect whether the cause lies in tool handling, instructions, or grading. Rerun the unchanged baseline and candidate on the old and new cases, compare regressions individually, keep ordinary-traffic estimates separate from deliberately difficult cases, and use fresh held-out cases after tuning. Repeating existing cases cannot supply the missing kind of situation. Grounding: §§2–4 and 6, especially the operational-failure row and instruction to turn confirmed failures into new cases.

**From B alone:** The old score describes a narrower domain than the new failure. If customers begin correcting delivery dates in later turns and the original set contains only single-turn requests, add such conversations and require the final answer to use the corrected date. Review full outputs and actions, examine the failure cause, and compare candidate and baseline case by case. Separate representative tickets from severe stress cases, inspect affected categories, and refresh held-out examples after incorporating a failure into development. More repetitions of single-turn cases test consistency within that case type, not dialogue coverage. Grounding: “First make ‘correct’ concrete,” “A single successful answer can hide inconsistency,” and “Turn scores into a decision.”

## Primary-source and executable verification

- [Anthropic’s evaluation article](https://www.anthropic.com/research/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) supports task/trial/grader distinctions, observing outcomes rather than accepting claims, combining graders, and the initial 20–50-task recommendation. A correctly presents that number as starting guidance, not certification.
- [τ-bench v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1), §3 and §5.1, confirms the final-state reward can miss absent confirmation, and reports the stated above-60% single-attempt versus below-25% all-eight result. Both correctly distinguish the success criteria and historical simulation scope.
- [NIST’s proportion intervals](https://itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section2/prc241.htm) includes an exact binomial interval construction. A’s one-sided zero-failure bound is an adaptation of that method, not a quoted numerical NIST example. Independently recomputed 0.95^30 = 0.2146388 and 1−0.05^(1/30) = 0.0950339. Its sampling caveats are appropriate.
- [NIST AI RMF §3.1](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/3-sec-characteristics/) supports B’s realistic-use-condition and segmented-reporting explanation. [OpenAI’s workflow](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/evaluation-best-practices#design-your-eval-process) supports defining objectives, datasets, metrics, comparisons, and continuous updates.
- Inspected linked Python assets. A’s `--demo` produced six recorded attempts with all quality judgments pending. B’s `--self-test` produced 6/18 passing decision checks and pending human review, as stated. Neither measures model quality. B’s runner will overwrite a reused output filename, so users must actually follow its instruction to preserve old outputs; this is a small implementation caveat, not a conceptual error.

**Major issues:** none. Confidence in conceptual scores: high. Presentation checks cover sampled desktop/mobile layouts and controls, not a full assistive-technology audit.
