## Transfer probe: attacker substitutes their public key

**From A alone:** A stranger who receives the attacker’s substituted key encrypts to the attacker. The attacker has the matching private key and can recover the message; the intended recipient’s private key does not match. This does not break the encryption mathematics. A name or email attached to the key is insufficient. Obtain the recipient’s fingerprint through an already trusted channel and compare it before sending. A signature answers an authorization question but does not make content secret; trusting a purported owner still needs an authentic key. Grounding: “A public key still needs an owner,” supported by the earlier distinction between sender and recipient capabilities.

**From B alone:** Encryption can work perfectly while addressing the wrong recipient. The public key substitution changes who possesses the matching decryption capability. Verify the key through an independently trusted channel or a trusted party’s signed identification. A signature checked against another attacker-supplied key establishes consistency with that key, not Alice’s identity. Neither a larger modulus nor randomized encoding fixes this identity problem: those address different attack mechanisms. Grounding: “The public key must really belong to the recipient,” together with “Why raw RSA is not enough.”

## Primary-source and calculation verification

- [RSA paper §§V–VIII](https://people.csail.mit.edu/rivest/Rsapaper.pdf) contains n=2773, e=17, d=157 and 920→948→920. Its proof supports B’s prime-remainder explanation, including the divisible-input case; §IX distinguishes factoring from a fully proved characterization of all attacks. Both reports accurately limit historical guidance.
- Independently recomputed A: 42^7 mod 143 = 81; 81^103 mod 143 = 42; 81^7 mod 143 = 16. Checked encrypt/decrypt recovery for all 143 inputs. Independently recomputed B: 920^17 mod 2773 = 948; 948^157 mod 2773 = 920; 948^17 mod 2773 = 1207. Checked recovery for all 2773 inputs, matching B’s claim. These computations establish only toy correctness.
- [RFC 8017 §5](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8017.html#section-5) supports the distinction between a mathematical primitive and secure scheme. [§7.1.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8017.html#section-7.1.1) places random seed generation before the RSA operation, as A states and B summarizes. Their deterministic-message comparison explains a real failure of bare RSA without treating arbitrary added randomness as sufficient.
- [RFC 9180 §§4–5](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9180.html#section-4) supports the encapsulation/shared-secret/derived-key explanation. [§9.7](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9180.html#section-9.7) supports the limitations on recipient-compromise forward secrecy and message-length protection. In modes with a pre-shared secret, that additional secret matters; the reports describe the ordinary public-key/basic-mode construction, so this is not a consequential error in context.
- [GNU Privacy Handbook](https://www.gnupg.org/gph/en/manual.html), “Importing a public key,” supports A’s fingerprint check through a channel known to reach the true owner. [NIST’s definition](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/public_key_cryptography) supports B’s computational-infeasibility framing. The RSA paper §IV supports B’s public-directory identity concern.
- In the rendered A demo, changed input to 142 and ran public search: it recovered 142 after 143 trials, correctly showing that public repetition can also recover particular special inputs. Reset restored 42. No script errors or missing images were observed across the two reports.

**Major issues:** none. A’s incomplete explanation of the inverse relationship is a depth/continuity limitation, not a factual error. Confidence in the narrow preference: moderate; readers who prioritize hands-on exploration over the extra generality argument may prefer A. Presentation checks are not a full assistive-technology audit.
