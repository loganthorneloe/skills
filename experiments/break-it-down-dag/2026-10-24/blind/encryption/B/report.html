How can a public key keep a message private?

Sources and what they establish

Public key cryptography (PKC)↗

NIST Computer Security Resource Center · Technical glossary · National Institute of Standards and Technology · accessed October 24, 2026

NIST, the US agency that publishes cryptographic standards, collects definitions from its technical publications. Its definition drawn from SP 800-56B explains the essential distinction: related keys can have different capabilities. A definition states the security requirement; it does not prove that every implementation meets it.

A Method for Obtaining Digital Signatures and Public-Key Cryptosystems↗

Ronald Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman · Research paper · Communications of the ACM · February 1978

The inventors of RSA, then working at MIT, explain its arithmetic and give the numerical example used below (§§V–VIII). Their mathematics remains useful for understanding reversibility. Their historical speed estimates and key-size advice are not present-day security guidance.

PKCS #1: RSA Cryptography Specifications Version 2.2↗

Kathleen Moriarty, editor; Burt Kaliski, Jakob Jonsson, and Andreas Rusch · Informational RFC 8017 · IETF · November 2016

This republication of RSA Laboratories’ specification defines the operations software must perform. Sections 3–5 specify keys and arithmetic; §7.1 specifies randomized RSA encryption. It distinguishes a mathematical operation from a complete encryption scheme. It is a specification, not a test of a particular app.

Hybrid Public Key Encryption↗

Richard Barnes, Karthikeyan Bhargavan, Benjamin Lipp, and Christopher Wood · Informational RFC 9180 · IRTF Crypto Forum Research Group · February 2022

The authors, affiliated with Cisco, Inria, and Cloudflare in the document, specify HPKE directly. Sections 4–5 explain combining public-key operations with shared-key encryption; §9 identifies limits. This describes one construction, not every messaging app.

The direct answer

The public key lets anyone perform the forward calculation. The private key supplies information that makes the reverse calculation efficient. Knowing the forward calculation does not necessarily tell you how to reverse it efficiently.

Both keys are data, usually stored in files or managed by software. They are mathematically related, but they are not interchangeable. With a sound encryption scheme and suitable keys, deriving the secret from the published information is computationally infeasible: the best known attacks require impractical resources under the scheme’s assumptions. That is a claim about difficulty, not logical impossibility. NIST’s definition↗.

First, a message is already numbers

A file is a sequence of bits: values of zero or one. Groups of bits represent letters, pixels, or other data. You can also interpret those groups as ordinary integers. Changing a representation does not hide anything: someone who knows the representation can read it.

Encryption adds a key-controlled transformation. The readable input is called plaintext; the transformed output is ciphertext. Decryption recovers the input. The algorithm is the published procedure; a key is data supplied to that procedure. The secret is not how the software works.

A shared-key, or symmetric, scheme uses the same secret key to encrypt and decrypt. Publishing that key would let everyone decrypt. Public-key encryption avoids publishing the decryption key. The challenge is to construct two capabilities that remain different even when an observer knows every step of the algorithm.

Why the direction of a calculation matters

Multiplying 47 by 59 gives 2773. Starting with 2773 and finding those two factors is a different task. A prime is a whole number greater than one divisible only by itself and one; both 47 and 59 are prime. Factoring means recovering the numbers whose product you were given.

These small numbers are easy in either direction. RSA uses carefully generated, much larger primes. Multiplication remains efficient; recovering their factors is believed to be difficult for sufficiently large, properly generated values with classical computers. The recipient starts with the factors and therefore never needs to solve that reverse problem. Factoring would break RSA, but we should not conclude that every conceivable attack must factor the number. The original paper explicitly leaves that broader equivalence unproved (§IX.D).

Multiplication alone does not encrypt a message. RSA uses the hidden factors to construct two exponents—numbers that tell you how many times to multiply a value by itself. One exponent becomes public; the other remains secret.

Watch a complete numerical example

One more operation makes the arithmetic work: modulo, meaning “keep the remainder after division.” For example, 23 modulo 7 is 2 because 23 = 3 × 7 + 2. RSA repeatedly multiplies numbers while keeping remainders, so it does not need to store the enormous full powers.

The RSA paper’s §VIII uses these values. They are deliberately small and insecure, so the calculation can be checked.

For a message represented by 920, the sender calculates 920 raised to power 17, keeping its remainder after division by 2773. The result is 948. Only 948 travels as the ciphertext in this simplified example. The receiver raises 948 to power 157 with the same remainder rule and gets 920.

Why does 157 reverse 17? Calculate (47 − 1) × (59 − 1) = 2668. Then 17 × 157 = 2669 = 2668 + 1. A number-theoretic property makes that relationship restore every allowed input when using the remainder rule. More specifically, for a number not divisible by 47, its 46th power leaves remainder 1 when divided by 47. The corresponding exponent for 59 is 58. Because 2668 is a multiple of both 46 and 58, raising a message to power 2668 + 1 restores its remainder for both primes. That determines the original value between 0 and 2772. Inputs divisible by either prime also work: their remainder for that prime stays zero. The factors make this relationship easy to arrange. The public key contains 2773 and 17, but does not directly supply 2668 or 157.

What if you apply the public operation twice?

This original calculation isolates the mistaken assumption that the public key can also decrypt:

92017 mod 2773 = 948
94817 mod 2773 = 1207
948157 mod 2773 = 920

The second application of the public operation does not undo the first. The private exponent does. Some particular inputs can behave differently; the important requirement is recovering arbitrary messages.

An observer could still try all 2773 possible inputs until one encrypts to 948. That succeeds here. At realistic sizes, schemes must resist much more effective attacks too; “try every input” is not the only possibility.

Why raw RSA is not enough

Suppose the only possible messages are YES and NO, and the same message always produces the same ciphertext. Anyone with the public key can encrypt both guesses, compare them with the intercepted result, and learn your answer. They never need the private key. Merely increasing the key size does not fix that problem.

What does the RSA specification require beyond arithmetic?

RSA-OAEP is such a scheme. It encodes the message using fresh randomness before applying RSA. Repeating the same message can then produce different ciphertexts; encrypting a guess once no longer supplies a reliable comparison. OAEP also specifies structure and checks. Random bytes appended arbitrarily are not a substitute. RFC 8017 §7.1.1↗.

How this becomes private files and messages

Real systems commonly use hybrid encryption. A public-key operation establishes a fresh shared secret; a symmetric algorithm uses a derived key to protect the message efficiently. In HPKE, the sender uses the recipient’s public key and fresh randomness to produce that secret plus a public value called an encapsulation. The recipient combines the encapsulation with the private key to recover the secret. The encapsulation is not the secret itself.

Both sides derive matching encryption keys from the secret. The sender encrypts the file and transmits its ciphertext and encapsulation. The receiver recovers the key and decrypts. HPKE’s authenticated encryption also detects ciphertext modifications. Its basic mode does not identify the sender: anyone can encrypt to that public key. RFC 9180 §§4–5↗.

The public key must really belong to the recipient

Imagine you want to send Alice a file, but an attacker replaces Alice’s published public key with the attacker’s own. Your software encrypts perfectly—to the wrong recipient. The attacker can read the file because it has the matching private key. Nothing about the arithmetic detects the name substitution.

You therefore need a trustworthy connection between Alice’s identity and the key, such as comparing the key through an already trusted channel or relying on a trusted party’s signed identification of it. A digital signature is a separate operation: a private key creates evidence that a corresponding public key can verify. Verification establishes a relationship to that key; deciding whose key it is still needs trusted information. The RSA paper discusses this public-directory problem in §IV.

Protecting the private key remains essential. HPKE does not protect old recorded messages if the recipient’s long-term private key is later stolen. Nor does encryption automatically hide message length. RFC 9180 §9.7↗.

Publishing the encryption capability is safe only when it leaves the decryption task impractical for outsiders, the complete scheme protects predictable messages, and the public key has been authenticated. The sender already knows its own message; the point is that other holders of the public key do not thereby gain access to it.
