Attention in transformer language models.

Sources

Attention Is All You Need↗ Ashish Vaswani, Noam Shazeer, and colleagues · Conference paper, NeurIPS (arXiv preprint, version 7 read) · June 2017

The Google team that invented the transformer wrote this paper, so it is the primary definition of the attention formula, the masking rule, and the layer design used below. Its experiments are on translation between languages, not chat, and its training-cost figures are the authors' own estimates. One English–French score is printed differently in two places, so this report does not use it.

Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models↗ Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish, and colleagues · Research preprint, arXiv (OpenAI) · January 2020

The authors trained hundreds of next-word-prediction models themselves and measured how their error changes with size, data, and computing budget, and how transformers compare with the older recurrent design. The models top out near 1.5 billion parameters, and later work revised the best way to split a budget between model size and data, so the exact slopes should not be read as current.

Language Models are Few-Shot Learners↗ Tom B. Brown, Benjamin Mann, and colleagues · Conference paper, NeurIPS (arXiv preprint) · May 2020

This is OpenAI's own description of GPT-3, the model family ChatGPT grew out of. It is used only for GPT-3's architecture and size, which it states in a table.

Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback↗ Long Ouyang, Jeff Wu, and colleagues · Conference paper, NeurIPS (arXiv preprint) · March 2022

The team that built InstructGPT, the direct predecessor of ChatGPT's training method, describes the procedure and reports which answers human raters preferred. The raters were about 40 hired contractors judging prompts sent to OpenAI's service, so the preferences are theirs rather than everyone's.

ChatGPT: Optimizing Language Models for Dialogue↗ OpenAI · Product announcement, OpenAI blog · November 2022

OpenAI's launch post is first-hand about how ChatGPT was trained, but it is brief and does not describe the underlying model's architecture. The live page refused automated access, so it was read from an Internet Archive copy↗ taken on launch day.

The short answer

For every word-piece in a text, attention computes a new vector that is a weighted average of information from the other word-pieces, where the weights come from how well each one matches what the current word-piece is looking for. All of those averages are computed at once with a few matrix multiplications. That last property is what mattered for ChatGPT. Older models had to read text one position at a time; attention lets a model process every position of a long text in parallel on graphics processors, so it became practical to train very large models on enormous amounts of text, and those models kept getting better as they grew. ChatGPT is one of those large models, given extra training afterward on human feedback so that it answers requests instead of just continuing text.

What a language model does

A language model is a program that, given some text, outputs a probability for every possible next piece of text. The pieces are called tokens: whole words, parts of words, or punctuation, drawn from a fixed vocabulary. The scaling-laws models, for example, used a vocabulary of 50,257 tokens and were trained to predict each token from the 1,024 tokens before it. To generate text, the program picks a likely next token, appends it, and repeats.

The program cannot do arithmetic on words directly, so each token is first replaced by a list of numbers, a vector, looked up from a learned table. In the original transformer each vector had 512 numbers; in GPT-3 it has 12,288. "Learned" means the numbers start random and are adjusted automatically during training: the model makes predictions on real text, a formula measures how wrong they were, and every adjustable number (a parameter) is nudged slightly in the direction that would have made the prediction less wrong. Repeat that across billions of tokens and the parameters come to encode useful patterns.

The hard part is context. The right next word after "The trophy did not fit in the suitcase because it was too" depends on "trophy" and "suitcase", many tokens back. Each token's vector, on its own, knows nothing about its neighbors. Something has to move information between positions.

The problem attention replaced

Before 2017, the standard way to move information between positions was a recurrent network. It walks through the text left to right, keeping one running summary vector, and at each position it combines the summary so far with the current token to produce the next summary. The transformer paper names the cost directly: each summary is a function of the previous one, and this "inherently sequential nature precludes parallelization within training examples." Position 500 cannot be computed until positions 1 through 499 are done, no matter how many processors are available. Information from early tokens also has to be carried through hundreds of successive summaries to reach a late one.

Attention takes a different route. Instead of passing a summary along a chain, every position looks directly at every other position and pulls in what it needs.

What attention computes

Attention starts from the token vectors, one per position, stacked as the rows of a matrix X. It multiplies X by three learned weight matrices to get three new matrices with one row per position:

  • a query row for each position, Q = X·WQ, which encodes what that position is looking for;
  • a key row for each position, K = X·WK, which encodes what that position offers to be found by;
  • a value row for each position, V = X·WV, which holds the information that position hands over if it is chosen.

Nobody writes down what "looking for" means. The three weight matrices are parameters, so training adjusts them until the queries and keys that end up matching are ones that help predict the next token.

Then the paper's Equation 1 does the rest:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q·KT / √dk ) · V

Read it from the inside out. Q·KT is an ordinary matrix multiplication whose entry in row i, column j is the dot product of position i's query with position j's key. A dot product is large when two vectors point the same way, so this entry is a score for how well position j matches what position i wants. With n tokens, this is an n-by-n table of scores.

Each score is divided by √dk, the square root of the length of the key vectors. The authors explain why in a footnote: if the numbers in a query and a key are random with variance 1, their dot product has variance dk, so longer vectors produce larger scores. Very large scores push the next step into a regime where training barely changes anything, and dividing by the square root cancels that growth.

Softmax is then applied to each row separately. It exponentiates every score and divides by the row's total, which turns any list of numbers into positive weights that sum to 1, with bigger scores getting a disproportionately bigger share. Row i now says how much of each position to use, for example 62% of position 2, 20% of position 5, and a little of everything else.

The final multiplication by V applies those weights. Row i of the output is the weighted sum of all the value rows, using row i's weights. So each position comes out holding a blend of information gathered from the positions it matched best. The paper's own one-line summary is that the output is "a weighted sum of the values," with each weight computed by comparing the query with the corresponding key.

With the defaults, it puts 62% of its weight on dog, so its output vector is now mostly "noun-like dog information". That is the sense in which attention lets a pronoun pick up what it refers to. Three things are worth trying. Move the multiplier to 0 and every weight becomes equal, so the output is a flat average and the query no longer matters. Push it to 4 and nearly all the weight lands on dog; softmax with large scores behaves almost like picking a single winner, which is the effect the √dk division keeps in check as vectors get longer. Finally, choose barked as the query: with the mask on, positions 4 and 5 vanish from its table, which leads to the next point.

Why the model is not allowed to look ahead

A model trained to predict the next token would learn nothing if each position could simply look at the next token. The transformer prevents this inside attention itself, by "masking out (setting to −∞) all values in the input of the softmax which correspond to illegal connections." Because the exponential of −∞ is 0, every later position gets a weight of exactly zero, and position i can use only positions 1 through i. This is what the "Mask (opt.)" box in the figure does. Models like GPT use only this masked form, which the scaling-laws paper calls a "decoder-only" transformer.

The mask also explains a training shortcut. Since every position is scored at once and each can see only its past, one pass over a 2,048-token passage produces 2,048 next-token predictions in parallel, each checked against the real next token.

From one attention to a full model

A single set of query, key, and value matrices can only express one notion of "matching". The transformer therefore runs several attentions side by side, called heads, each with its own three projection matrices and shorter vectors, and joins their outputs with one more learned matrix. The paper says this lets the model attend "to information from different representation subspaces at different positions," whereas a single head would average them together. The original model used 8 heads of 64 numbers each, chosen so that the total cost matches one full-width head.

Attention is only half of each layer. After it, every position separately passes through a small two-layer network of ordinary matrix multiplications (a feed-forward network), which transforms the information it just gathered. Each sub-layer's output is added back to its input rather than replacing it, which keeps the original token information available to later layers. Because attention treats its inputs as an unordered set (shuffling the rows would shuffle the output the same way), the model also adds a position-dependent vector to each token's vector at the start, so "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" look different. Layers are stacked, and each layer's attention works on the blended vectors the previous layer produced, so later layers can combine information that was gathered in several hops.

GPT-3 is this same design made very large. Its paper reports 96 layers, each with 96 heads of 128 numbers, token vectors of 12,288 numbers, a context of 2,048 tokens, and 175 billion parameters in total, trained on 300 billion tokens. It keeps the GPT-2 architecture except that some layers attend only to a nearby window of positions (“locally banded sparse attention”) instead of to all earlier positions.

Why attention made ChatGPT possible

Attention did not make models smart by itself. What it changed was what was affordable to build, and three properties combined.

It turns sequence processing into parallel matrix work

The transformer paper compares a self-attention layer with a recurrent layer on three measures. The table below is rebuilt from its Table 1, where n is the number of tokens and d is the vector length.

The middle column is the one that matters for hardware. A recurrent layer needs n steps in a row, so a 2,000-token passage is 2,000 dependent steps. Attention needs a fixed number of steps regardless of length, because the whole n-by-n score table comes out of one matrix multiplication. Graphics processors are built to do exactly that kind of large, independent arithmetic, and the paper notes that dot-product attention is fast “since it can be implemented using highly optimized matrix multiplication code.” The last column matters for learning: any two positions are one step apart, so the training signal connecting an early word to a late prediction does not have to pass through hundreds of intermediate summaries.

The payoff showed up immediately. On English-to-German translation, the larger transformer scored 28.4 BLEU (a standard translation-quality score), more than 2 points above every earlier published result including combinations of several models, after 3.5 days of training on 8 GPUs. The authors estimate its training at 2.3×1019 floating-point operations, the same as the best single recurrent system's estimate and less than a third of the strongest combined convolutional system's.

It actually uses long context

This is the practical meaning of the one-step path length. A recurrent model must hold everything it has read in one fixed-size summary vector, so distant text fades; attention can reach back to token 1 from token 1,000 as easily as from token 2.

It keeps improving as it grows

Put the three properties together and the path to GPT-3 is clear. Attention made training parallel enough to spread across large numbers of processors at once, the architecture used long context well, and measured error fell smoothly with every increase in size, which made spending far more on a larger model a predictable bet rather than a gamble. GPT-3, at 175 billion parameters, had ten times more parameters than any earlier non-sparse language model, according to its paper.

The last step was not attention

A model trained only to predict internet text continues text; it does not reliably answer you. The InstructGPT paper puts it plainly: predicting the next token on a webpage is a different goal from following instructions helpfully and safely. OpenAI's fix was additional training on human judgments, shown below.

The effect was large. Raters preferred answers from a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT model over answers from the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, even though, as the paper notes, the two have the same architecture and differ only in this extra training. ChatGPT's launch post says it was trained “using the same methods as InstructGPT,” with conversations as data, and that it “is fine-tuned from a model in the GPT-3.5 series, which finished training in early 2022.” OpenAI has not published the GPT-3.5 architecture, so the claim that ChatGPT is a transformer rests on its descent from the documented GPT-3 and InstructGPT models rather than on a stated specification.

So the division of labor is this. Attention, run in parallel across many heads and layers, is the mechanism that made it affordable to train a model large enough to absorb the patterns of a huge amount of text. Human-feedback training then shaped that model into something that responds to requests.

Limits and what they imply

Cost grows with the square of length

The score table is n by n, so doubling the context quadruples attention's work and memory, as Table 1's O(n²·d) shows. This is why a model can read only a limited amount of text at once, and why GPT-3 already used local windows in some layers.

Attention weights are hints, not explanations

Patterns like the “its” figure are striking, but the authors hedge them and chose the examples. A head's weights show where information was drawn from in one layer, not why the final prediction came out as it did.

Order has to be added

Attention itself ignores word order; the model only knows order through the position vectors added at the start.

The broader implication is that attention's importance is mostly about what it made cheap. It replaced a computation that had to run one step at a time with one that runs as a few large matrix multiplications, and that changed the economics of training. The capabilities people see in ChatGPT come from what that affordable scale allowed the model to learn, plus the human-feedback training layered on top, not from attention alone.
