The short answer

Two sounds at the same spot add, so a speaker playing the exact mirror image of the noise leaves almost nothing at your ear. The mirror image must land within a few degrees of the right point in the wave's cycle. Any delay left in the electronics is a tiny slice of a slow wave's cycle but a large slice of a fast one's, so headphones that remove 20–30 dB of a low engine hum do almost nothing above about 1 kHz. A hum also repeats steadily; a voice carries much of its meaning in the range from 1 kHz up to about 4 kHz and changes every syllable, so the ear cups have to block it physically.

Sound adds up, so it can also subtract

Sound is a rise and fall in air pressure travelling at about 343 metres per second; the rises per second are its frequency in hertz (Hz). Where two sounds meet, the pressures add, which is called superposition. Shift one wave by half a cycle so its peaks fall on the other's troughs, and equal waves cancel. That shift is measured as phase, 0° to 360° per cycle, so a perfect mirror image is 180° out of phase. The added sound is anti-noise, and the cancelling is destructive interference.

What is inside the headphone

Microphones hear the noise, a chip inverts it, and the music speaker plays the anti-noise. A feedforward microphone outside the cup hears noise early, giving the chip a head start; a feedback microphone inside hears what is left and pushes it toward zero. Hybrid designs use both. The cups also block sound physically (passive isolation); active noise cancellation (ANC) is the electronic part on top.

The anti-noise must be almost perfect

Anti-noise slightly too quiet or slightly late lets part of the noise survive. Skovorodnikov's chart shows how much, as it misses on loudness (horizontal) and phase (vertical).

A decibel (dB) is a log scale: −10 dB is a tenth of the sound power, heard as roughly half as loud. A clearly audible 10 dB reduction, Skovorodnikov writes, needs "a magnitude mismatch of up to 2dB and phase deviation from the ideal of 180° up to 20°." Adding two waves shows why: correctly sized anti-noise that misses by φ leaves 2 − 2·cos φ of the power, −29 dB at 2°, −15 dB at 10°, 0 dB at 60°.

Why the same delay hurts high pitches more

Microphone, chip and speaker each take time; Skovorodnikov's example microphone alone adds 24 microseconds (µs). The feedforward head start offsets some of it, but a headphone is so small that the head start is tiny, "limiting the available processing time window," as Shi and colleagues note. A remaining timing error τ becomes a phase error of 360° × f × τ, growing with frequency f.

Suppose 50 µs of error remains. For a 100 Hz hum, whose cycle lasts 10,000 µs, that is 1.8°, and about 30 dB is still removed. For a 2 kHz consonant, with a 500 µs cycle, it is 36°, and only about 4 dB goes. At 3.3 kHz it reaches 60° and cancellation stops. Hence "lower system latency improves the high-frequency limit," in Skovorodnikov's words; halving the delay doubles the ceiling but keeps the pattern.

The feedback loop has to back off at high frequencies

The feedback microphone's signal runs through the chip and speaker back into the same microphone, a loop. With delay in the loop, too much strength reinforces the sound instead of correcting it, and the headphone can howl. So the controller cuts its strength at high frequencies and slightly amplifies noise in the hand-over band, the waterbed effect.

What measurements of real headphones show

RTINGS measured three flagship headphones on an artificial head with ANC on and off, using pink noise, a steady hiss of every frequency. Lower is quieter.

Electronics own the bass, where long waves pass through cup materials easily; cups own the treble. In RTINGS's words, "ANC doesn't improve upper-mid and treble range attenuation over passive isolation."

Why an engine hum is the easy case

A hum is low, and it repeats. Engines, propellers and fans turn at a steady rate, so their sound is a few steady tones; Shi and colleagues describe aircraft cabin noise at "the fundamental and harmonics of the blade passing frequency," and report about 30 dB of adaptive cancellation of such tones in a duct. Each cycle looks like the last, so the system only has to keep its inverted copy lined up, and at 100 Hz that copy tolerates 50 µs of error.

Why voices get through

A voice fails on both counts. Much of it sits too high, and it never holds still.

Monson and colleagues recount that early telephone research found about 95% of speech understood with everything above 4 kHz removed, which is why phone lines carried 300 Hz to 3.4 kHz. Most of that band lies above the measured 1 kHz ceiling, where delay, the waterbed and the ear's own resonance around 2–3 kHz, which Skovorodnikov names as a limit on filter fit, all work against cancellation. The cancelled region also shrinks as frequency rises, as Shi and colleagues note for car cabins; at 2 kHz, with a 17 cm wavelength, the gap between microphone and eardrum starts to matter.

The lowest harmonics do fall in the cancelled band but shift too fast to track. RTINGS found one popular model took "around three full seconds to reach their maximum attenuation" of a steady tone, and Shi and colleagues note most headphones use fixed filters because their chips cannot run better adaptive methods fast enough. Speech comes through muffled by the cups, not cancelled.

What this means when you wear them

The headphones are two systems: electronics remove steady low sounds such as engines and air conditioning, and the cups' seal blocks most of speech, so a poor seal lets voices in however good the electronics are. Faster parts raise the ceiling, but no delay is zero and a changing sound cannot be predicted like a hum. Wind on the outside microphones is a further limit: RTINGS found it can make some headphones louder with ANC on.
