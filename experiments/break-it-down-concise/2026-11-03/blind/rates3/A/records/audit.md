# Source audit records

Claims
- C1: A central bank directly controls only a short-term interest rate; it does so by setting rates it pays or charges banks (administered rates), and market rates follow.
- C2: Higher policy rates reduce spending by households and firms (cash flow of borrowers, reward for saving, lower asset values, costlier investment, stronger currency).
- C3: Lower demand relative to the economy's capacity eases wage and price pressure, so inflation falls; inflation expectations and import prices also matter.
- C4: The peak effect on output arrives after roughly a year, and on inflation after roughly two years (US estimates: prices start falling after ~18–24 months).
- C5: The lag comes from contracts and slow pass-through to retail rates, a chain of inventory/order/production/employment adjustments, and sticky prices and wages.
- C6: Size: rough magnitudes of a 1 percentage point rise.
- C7: Limits: supply shocks and prices that respond little to domestic demand (food, energy, some services) are only weakly affected.
- C8: Lags are variable and may be shorter when policy is signaled in advance or moves are large.

### The transmission mechanism of monetary policy
- url: https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/quarterly-bulletin/1999/q2/the-transmission-mechanism-of-monetary-policy; text read from: https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1999/the-transmission-mechanism-of-monetary-policy (PDF, Quarterly Bulletin pp. 161–170)
- byline: Bank of England staff under the guidance of the Monetary Policy Committee · central bank report · Bank of England Quarterly Bulletin · May 1999
- verdicts: C1 pass; C2 pass; C3 pass; C4 pass (as the Bank's model and its summary of evidence for industrial economies); C5 pass; C6 conditional (model simulation, illustrative only, policy reversed after one year, UK 1990s); C7 conditional (mentions other influences on prices; use Fed for supply shocks); C8 conditional (says lags "long, variable and uncertain"; predates forward-guidance era)
- use: Introduction and figure p.161; Part I sections on market rates, individuals, firms, time-lags (p.167), GDP and inflation (output gap), inflation expectations and real rates (p.168); Part II Charts 1–2 and text (p.170)
- credibility: Written by the staff of the institution that sets UK rates, under the guidance of its rate-setting committee, describing its own model and operating procedure; firsthand access.
- contribution: Step-by-step causal chain from official rate to market rates, spending, demand versus capacity, wages and prices; explains why each step takes time; model simulation of a 1pp rise for one year: GDP falls 0.2–0.35% at ~5 quarters, inflation little changed in year 1, falls 0.2–0.4pp at ~9 quarters, not back to base after 3 years.
- limits: 1999 UK context (most mortgages floating-rate then; US mortgages mostly fixed). Simulation is from one macro model under two policy-rule assumptions, explicitly "not a forecast" and no probability on the range. Mechanism description endures; specific magnitudes are historical. Rights: Bank of England copyright; re-use requires permission ("typically grants permission for non-commercial re-use"), so charts are linked, not embedded. No errata found (searched).
- read: full (all 10 pages) · via BoE PDF

### How Quickly Do Prices Respond to Monetary Policy?
- url: https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/economic-letter/2024/04/how-quickly-do-prices-respond-to-monetary-policy; text read from: https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/el2024-10.pdf
- byline: Zoë Arnaut and Leila Bengali · research note · FRBSF Economic Letter 2024-10, Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco · April 8, 2024
- verdicts: C4 pass (US estimate); C6 pass (headline PCE price level ~2.5% lower four years after a 1pp rise); C7 pass (categories differ; food and energy driven by global markets); C8 conditional (notes debate, does not resolve it)
- use: pp. 2–4, Figures 1 and 3, text on timing
- credibility: Fed research economists estimating responses of 136 PCE price categories to Romer–Romer policy shocks (updated by Wieland and Yang) with local projections; original analysis.
- contribution: In US data, a 1pp funds-rate rise starts pushing the most responsive prices below baseline after ~18 months; headline prices go negative after a little over 24 months and show month-to-month declines after ~18 months; headline PCE price level ~2.5% below baseline after four years.
- limits: Short non-peer-reviewed letter; estimates rely on historical shock identification; 95% bands are wide (Figure 3); grouping by response size not speed. Rights: "Permission to reprint ... must be obtained in writing" — link figures, do not embed. No correction found (searched).
- read: full (5 pages) · via FRBSF PDF

### Big Shocks Travel Fast: Why Policy Lags May Be Shorter Than You Think
- url: https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/waller20230713a.htm; text read from: same
- byline: Christopher J. Waller, Governor, Federal Reserve Board · speech · Money Marketeers of New York University · July 13, 2023
- verdicts: C4 pass (states the 12–24 month rule of thumb for real effects); C5 pass (lists adjustment costs, sticky prices and wages, nominal contracts, habit persistence, option value of waiting); C8 conditional (a policymaker's argument, not an estimate)
- use: section "Monetary Policy Lags"
- credibility: A sitting rate-setter describing the standard estimation approach and the Committee's discussion of lags.
- contribution: Explains impulse responses as hump-shaped (no cliff); gives reasons for sluggishness; argues forward guidance moved the 2-year Treasury yield from 0.25% (Sept 2021) to ~2% by March 2022, before hikes, shaving ~6 months off the lag; argues big shocks change behavior faster.
- limits: A speech with a policy argument; personal view. Rights: Board website, public domain.
- read: partial (opening and full "Monetary Policy Lags" section; outlook section not read)
