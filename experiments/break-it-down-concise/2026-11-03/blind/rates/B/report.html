How raising interest rates slows inflation.

Sources

The transmission mechanism of monetary policy↗
Bank of England staff, for the Monetary Policy Committee · Central bank report, Bank of England Quarterly Bulletin · May 1999

The staff of the central bank that sets UK rates trace each link from its rate to inflation and report timing from its own forecasting model. It describes the UK of 1999, when most mortgages were floating-rate, and its timing charts are illustrative model runs, not measurements.

How does the Federal Reserve affect inflation and employment?↗
Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System · Official FAQ, federalreserve.gov · Updated July 2024

The US central bank's own current description of its main tool and how it reaches spending, hiring and prices. It gives no numbers on size or timing.

Transmission Lags of Monetary Policy: A Meta-Analysis↗
Tomáš Havránek and Marek Rusnák · Peer-reviewed journal article, International Journal of Central Banking · December 2013

Two Czech National Bank economists pooled 198 estimates from 67 published studies covering 30 economies, so its average lag rests on many independent measurements. The studies end in 2010, and part of the spread reflects how long each study chose to look.

Five Facts about Prices: A Reevaluation of Menu Cost Models↗
Emi Nakamura and Jón Steinsson · Peer-reviewed journal article, Quarterly Journal of Economics · November 2008

Measures how often real prices change, using the individual price records behind the US consumer price index. The data cover the low-inflation years 1988–2005; prices change more often when inflation is high.

The short answer

A higher interest rate makes borrowing dearer and saving more rewarding, so households and businesses spend less. With fewer buyers, firms find it harder to raise prices and workers harder to win big raises, so prices rise more slowly. It takes a year or two because each step waits on the one before: loans reprice, spending plans change, firms cut production and hiring only after sales fall, and most prices are reset only every several months.

Inflation is the rate at which prices rise, say 5% a year. Slowing it usually means prices rise 2 or 3% a year instead, not that they fall.

What the central bank controls

A central bank sets only one very short-term rate, the rate at which banks lend to each other or borrow from it. In the US this is the federal funds rate, which the Fed describes as "the rate that banks pay for overnight borrowing." Banks pass it on, moving variable loan rates typically the same day and savings rates soon after, according to the Bank of England. Long-term rates, such as on fixed mortgages, follow where markets expect the short rate to go.

From a higher rate to less spending

Suppose the central bank raises its rate from 3% to 4% and is expected to hold it there.

A family with a $300,000 variable-rate mortgage now pays about $3,000 more interest a year, money that no longer goes on cars or holidays. Savers earn more by waiting. A furniture maker finds that a new workshop worth building at 3% is not worth borrowing for at 4%, so it postpones it and hires more slowly. Higher rates also lower house and share prices, so people feel poorer, and they strengthen the currency, making imports cheaper. Some people gain, such as retirees living on interest, but the net effect is less spending.

From less spending to slower price rises

The step that matters is demand relative to capacity. An economy can produce only so much with its workers and machines. When buyers want more than that, firms bid up pay for scarce workers and can raise prices without losing customers; the Bank of England notes those wage rises are what "some firms may be able to pass through into higher prices." Higher rates work by removing that pressure.

In the example, the furniture maker sells fewer sofas, so it raises prices 3% this year instead of the planned 8%. It stops hiring, so workers elsewhere accept smaller raises, which keeps down other firms' costs. Added across the economy, prices rise more slowly. Cheaper imports add a direct route.

Expectations decide how hard a given rise bites. What matters for borrowers is the real interest rate, roughly the rate minus expected inflation: borrowing at 4% while expecting 10% inflation is cheap, because you repay in money that has lost value. Expectations also enter pay deals: if everyone expects 5% inflation, contracts build in 5%. The Bank of England argues inflation settles at target only "once the target is credible."

Why it takes so long

The Bank of England puts the peak effect on output at up to about a year, and on inflation up to a further year.

The delay is a series of waits, each needing the last to finish. Fixed-rate borrowers pay the old rate until their term ends, and payments that do change take months to alter spending. Then weaker sales pile up stock in shops, which cut orders, so producers cut output, then jobs and pay. "All this takes time," the report says.

Last, prices themselves are reset rarely. In the records behind the US consumer price index, Nakamura and Steinsson found that, ignoring temporary sales, a typical item's regular price stayed unchanged for 8 to 11 months. So weaker demand reaches inflation gradually, as each price comes up for review. Firms reprice more often when inflation is high, so this step can be quicker during a surge.

Studies measuring real economies agree the wait is long. Havránek and Rusnák define the transmission lag as the months until prices have fallen furthest after a one-point rise.

Across 67 studies, the corrected average lag is 29 months, and the price level ends up about 0.9% lower at its deepest for each one-point rise. Large developed economies ran 25 to 50 months, and more developed financial systems, where borrowers can shield themselves longer, had longer lags.

What this means, and its limits

Because the effect peaks a year or two later, a central bank must act on its forecast of inflation, not today's figure; by the time inflation is visibly falling, it has probably done enough. The Bank of England calls these "long, variable and uncertain lags," and they shift with the share of fixed-rate borrowing and the pace of repricing.

Rates also work only on demand. The Bank of England says monetary policy "has little direct effect on the trend path of supply capacity." If prices rise because oil is scarce, a rate rise cannot make more oil; it can only cut spending elsewhere. The policy slows inflation by slowing the economy, at a cost in output and jobs.
