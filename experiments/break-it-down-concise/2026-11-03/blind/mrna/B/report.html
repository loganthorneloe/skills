The short answer

The shot delivers instructions, written in mRNA, for one harmless piece of the virus: its spike protein. Some of your cells read them and make spike for a short while. Your immune system treats that spike as foreign and builds antibodies and memory cells against it, so the real virus is recognized fast later. The mRNA cannot change your DNA: it works outside the nucleus where DNA is kept, your cells have no ordinary way to turn it back into DNA, and it is broken down within days to a couple of weeks.

The biology you need

Your DNA stays in each cell's nucleus. To make a protein, the cell copies one gene into a short-lived messenger RNA (mRNA), which moves out to the cytoplasm, where ribosomes read it and build the protein before the cell destroys it. Information flows one way, DNA to mRNA to protein. Vaccine mRNA uses the same machinery; it just comes from a lab.

What is in the shot

SARS-CoV-2, the virus behind COVID-19, uses its spike protein to enter cells, and antibodies that stick to spike can block it. So the vaccine's mRNA spells out spike. The FDA describes it as spike-encoding RNA "formulated in lipid nanoparticles": tiny fatty balls, about 100 nanometres across according to Pardi and colleagues, that protect the fragile mRNA and release it into cells near the injection site and in nearby lymph nodes.

Early lab-made mRNA set off the immune system's sensors for stray RNA, causing inflammation and little protein. Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman found that using chemically modified versions of the letter U, as your own cells often do, nearly abolished that reaction and raised protein output, work that won the 2023 Nobel Prize (the committee's Figure 2↗ illustrates it).

How making spike trains the immune system

Suppose the shot goes into your upper arm. A muscle cell reads the mRNA and makes spike, and the immune system, as the CDC puts it, recognizes the protein as foreign. Immune cells carry spike fragments to the lymph nodes in your armpit. Because spike is made inside cells, as in a real infection, it alerts both helper T cells, which coordinate the response, and killer T cells, which destroy infected cells (Pardi and colleagues).

The lasting part happens in lymph-node structures called germinal centres. B cells whose receptors fit spike multiply and mutate there, and T follicular helper cells select those that grip best; the winners become antibody-making plasma cells and long-lived memory B cells. Turner and colleagues found spike-binding germinal-centre B cells in all 14 people sampled, still high at least 12 weeks after the booster.

Evidence that it protects

When the real virus arrives, antibodies block its spike and memory cells respond faster than on a first encounter. In the trial, 36,621 participants without prior infection had 8 confirmed COVID-19 cases on vaccine and 162 on placebo from a week after dose 2, 95.0% efficacy by the FDA's count.

Why it does not change your DNA

To alter your DNA, vaccine mRNA would have to clear three barriers, and normal cell biology blocks each.

Reach the DNA

DNA sits inside the nucleus. Vaccine mRNA is released and read in the cytoplasm; the CDC states that it does "not enter the nucleus."

Become DNA

Turning RNA into DNA needs an enzyme, reverse transcriptase, that retroviruses such as HIV bring with them. Cells do not use one on ordinary mRNA, and the vaccine brings none.

Get spliced in

DNA does not insert itself; retroviruses carry a second enzyme, integrase, for that. The vaccine carries only spike instructions, which is also why it cannot cause infection.

Pardi and colleagues called mRNA a "non-infectious, non-integrating platform" for these reasons. It is also temporary, though not instant: Fertig and colleagues found traces of vaccine mRNA in blood for about two weeks.

The one published challenge is narrow. Aldén and colleagues bathed liver cancer cells in the vaccine and reported a DNA copy of its sequence within six hours, attributing it to LINE-1, a stretch of the human genome that can make reverse transcriptase. They wrote that "we do not know" whether that DNA entered the genome. Merchant replied that cancer cells in a dish differ from healthy liver in the relevant ways and received far more vaccine than a liver would. No source here shows vaccine sequence in a vaccinated person's genome.

What this means, and its limits

Protection is immune memory, not lingering mRNA. That is why it wanes as antibody levels fall over months, and why updated vaccines carry updated spike instructions as the virus changes. The trial is from 2020 and the original virus, and the lymph-node and blood studies are small; together they show how the mechanism works, not how strong protection is today.
