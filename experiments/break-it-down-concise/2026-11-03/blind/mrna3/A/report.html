How mRNA vaccines work.

How a short strand of genetic instructions teaches your immune system to recognize a virus, and why it does not touch your DNA.

Sources

    Two Karolinska Institutet immunology and genomics professors on the committee that judged the work wrote this as the formal case for the 2023 Nobel Prize. The short answer

The shot contains a copy of one gene-like instruction from the virus, the recipe for its spike protein, written as mRNA and wrapped in a tiny fat bubble. Some of your cells take it in and follow the recipe for a while, so they make spike protein and display it. Your immune system has never seen spike before. It treats spike as a sign of invasion and builds antibodies and specialized white blood cells against it, plus long-lived memory cells. When the real virus arrives, the memory cells recognize its spike and respond faster. The mRNA cannot change your DNA because it never goes where your DNA is kept. Turning it into DNA would also take enzymes the vaccine does not bring. Your cells break the mRNA down over days to weeks.

What your cells already do with mRNA

Every cell keeps its DNA in a walled-off compartment called the nucleus. DNA is the permanent library of recipes, one per protein. Proteins do most of the work in a body; some build structure, some speed up chemical reactions, some sit on a cell's surface as identifying tags.

A cell never sends the DNA itself out to be used. Instead it copies one recipe at a time into a short-lived molecule called messenger RNA, or mRNA. That copy leaves the nucleus and goes into the cytoplasm, the fluid-filled rest of the cell. There, ribosomes read the mRNA three letters at a time and link amino acids into the protein it spells out. Then the cell chops the mRNA up and recycles its parts. This is the "DNA makes RNA makes protein" flow from high-school biology. Your cells are making and destroying mRNA all day.

The flow runs in one direction. Nothing in the normal process writes mRNA back into the DNA library. That one fact is most of the answer to the DNA question.

What is in the shot

The vaccine has three working parts. The first is lab-made mRNA spelling out the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2, the virus that causes COVID-19. Spike is the knobbed protein that coats the virus's surface and lets it grab onto your cells. The mRNA carries the recipe for spike only, not the rest of the virus, so it cannot make a virus. The Pfizer-BioNTech trial describes its vaccine as a "lipid nanoparticle–formulated, nucleoside-modified RNA vaccine" that encodes the full-length spike, in a version locked into the shape it has before it attaches to a cell.

The second part is the wrapping. Bare mRNA injected into the body is quickly shredded by enzymes outside cells and barely gets in, as the Pardi review notes. So the mRNA is packed into lipid nanoparticles, which are bubbles about 100 nanometres across made of four kinds of fat-like molecules. One of them, an "ionizable" lipid, carries a positive charge in the acidic conditions where the particles are made, which holds the negatively charged mRNA. It turns neutral at the body's normal acidity, which makes it less toxic. After a cell swallows a particle into a small internal pouch, this lipid helps the mRNA escape into the cytoplasm.

The third part is a chemical change to the mRNA's letters, and it is what won the Nobel Prize. RNA is written in four bases, A, U, G and C. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman found that mRNA made in a lab set off alarms in immune cells. Those alarms are sensors called Toll-like receptors, which have evolved to spot foreign RNA from viruses and bacteria. The alarm caused inflammation and also made cells stop producing protein. In 2005 they showed that the RNA in your own cells carries chemically modified bases that these sensors ignore. When they built lab mRNA with a modified form of U, called pseudouridine, the alarm largely went quiet. In later work, cells also made far more protein from it.

Both licensed COVID-19 mRNA vaccines replace every U with a close relative, N1-methylpseudouridine, according to the Nobel Committee. That change let manufacturers put enough mRNA in each dose to get strong protection without unacceptable fever and aches. The sore arm and tiredness many people feel the next day are the immune system reacting to the shot; the modification lowers that reaction rather than abolishing it.

Inside one of your cells

After the injection into the upper arm, nanoparticles are taken up by cells near the injection site and in the nearby lymph nodes, which are the bean-shaped immune-cell hubs in your armpit. The illustration below follows one particle into one cell.

The Pardi review notes that the lab-made mRNA is designed to look like a finished mRNA from your own cells, complete with the cap at its front end and the long tail of A's at its back end that ribosomes expect. Because it has those features, the cell's ribosomes read it like any other message. The cell is not reprogrammed. It does what it always does with an mRNA it finds in its cytoplasm: makes the protein, then destroys the message.

How the immune system learns

Your immune system has two layers. The fast, general layer, called innate immunity, reacts to broad signs of trouble such as injury or foreign molecules. The slow, specific layer, called adaptive immunity, builds a response against one exact target, and it keeps a memory afterwards. Vaccines are aimed at the adaptive layer, but they need some innate alarm to get it started. The injection, the lipid particles and the RNA itself provide a mild version of that alarm.

Adaptive immunity depends on enormous variety. You carry millions of different B cells and T cells, and each one carries a receptor shaped to fit one particular molecular shape. Almost none of them fit spike. The vaccine's job is to put spike in front of all of them so the rare cells that do fit can find it, multiply and specialize. Three kinds of cell matter here.

B cells and antibodies

A B cell whose receptor fits spike gets activated, divides, and some of its descendants become factories for antibodies. Antibodies are Y-shaped proteins released into the blood that stick to spike. Antibodies that cover the parts of spike the virus uses to grab cells are called neutralizing, because they stop the virus from getting in.

Helper T cells

Cells that take up spike chop it into fragments and display them on their surface. Helper T cells (also called CD4+ T cells) that recognize those fragments give B cells the signals they need to keep refining their antibodies and to become long-lived.

Killer T cells

Because vaccinated cells make spike from the inside, they also display spike fragments the way virus-infected cells do. The Nobel Committee notes this gives mRNA vaccines the potential to train killer T cells (CD8+ T cells), which spot and destroy infected cells.

Memory

After the response peaks, most of these cells die off, but some memory B cells and memory T cells stay. If the real virus later shows up, they recognize its spike and mount a response much faster than the first time.

The refining step happens in germinal centres, small training sites inside lymph nodes where B cells compete and the ones whose antibodies fit spike best win. The Stanford lymph-node study found these sites active in vaccinated people's armpit lymph nodes for weeks, with vaccine mRNA and spike collected inside them. The slow fade of antigen there may be part of why the response gets strong.

The mechanism stays the same, but the protection it gives is not permanent, and it is weaker against a virus whose spike has changed.

Why it does not change your DNA

Here is the normal flow again, with the vaccine's entry point marked.

To travel the red path, three things would all have to happen, and the vaccine supplies none of them:

  1. The mRNA would have to be copied back into DNA, which takes an enzyme called a reverse transcriptase.
  2. That DNA would have to get through the wall of the nucleus.
  3. It would have to be cut into one of your chromosomes, which takes another enzyme, an integrase.

Retroviruses such as HIV insert themselves into DNA precisely because they carry their own reverse transcriptase and integrase. The vaccine mRNA encodes only spike. Each of the three barriers is worth a closer look.

Start with location. DNA sits behind the nuclear membrane, and the vaccine's mRNA is delivered to the cytoplasm and used there. The Nobel Committee points to this as an advantage over DNA vaccines, which must cross two membranes, the cell's outer wall and the nuclear wall, to work:

"mRNA-based vaccines only need to gain access the cell cytoplasm where translation takes place"

Nobel Committee scientific background, 2023

Next, chemistry. DNA and RNA are related but different molecules, and a reverse transcriptase is the enzyme that builds DNA using RNA as a template. The vaccine contains only the spike recipe and the fat particle, so it brings no such enzyme and no integrase. The review by the team that developed the technology calls mRNA "a non-infectious, non-integrating platform" with no risk of inserting itself into your genes, and the Nobel Committee says the delivered mRNA "cannot integrate into the host genome."

Finally, time. mRNA is built to be temporary, and the cell's normal machinery breaks it down. It does not vanish instantly, though. In the Stanford study, vaccine mRNA was still visible in lymph-node germinal centres 37 days after the second dose, and at lower but still noticeable levels at day 60. Spike protein was also present as late as day 60. That finding is about the immune training sites holding onto the material longer than many people expected. It does not show the mRNA entering a nucleus or DNA, and it came from seven people.

The study people cite as proof it changes DNA

In 2022 a Lund University group put the Pfizer-BioNTech vaccine on Huh7 cells, a line of liver cancer cells grown in dishes, at 0.5 to 2 micrograms per millilitre for 6 to 48 hours. They reported finding DNA copies of part of the vaccine sequence inside the cells within six hours, and changes in LINE-1. LINE-1 is a gene for a reverse transcriptase that sits in human DNA, a leftover of ancient virus-like elements that can occasionally copy itself. So human cells are not entirely without reverse transcriptase, and this is the real basis of the concern.

The study does not show that vaccination changes anyone's DNA, and its authors say so:

"At this stage, we do not know if DNA reverse transcribed from BNT162b2 is integrated into the cell genome."

Aldén et al., 2022, Discussion

A published critique pointed out that Huh7 cancer cells behave differently from healthy liver cells, including in how they handle RNA, and that the dose was justified using data on where the vaccine's fat molecules go rather than the mRNA itself. It also argues that a cell in a living body that makes spike becomes a target for the very immune cells the vaccine trains, which cells in a dish never face. I found no retraction of the study and no independent repeat of it. The fair reading is that one lab saw DNA copies of vaccine sequence in cancer cells in a dish, did not test whether any entered a chromosome, and the finding has not been shown in vaccinated people.

What this means, and what it does not settle

The mechanism is general. Changing which protein the mRNA spells out changes what the immune system learns, without changing the delivery system, which is why updated vaccines can be made quickly when the virus's spike changes. The same delivery approach is being tested for cancer vaccines, where killer T cells matter most.

Three limits are worth carrying away. Protection fades and is weaker against variants with altered spike, which is why boosters are given. The sources here describe how long vaccine material lasts in lymph nodes in only a handful of people, so "the mRNA is gone in a few days" is too strong; weeks is closer for those training sites. And the statement that the vaccine does not change DNA rests on how the system is built (it stays in the cytoplasm, it carries no enzymes to write or insert DNA, and it is degraded) rather than on studies that sequenced vaccinated people's genomes, which none of the sources here report. The one experiment pointing the other way was done in cancer cells and did not look for a change to the genome.
