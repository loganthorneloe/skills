Why app users scoring higher does not prove the app works.

The short answer

The students who choose a learning app are probably different from those who don't, and those differences affect exam scores whether or not the app does anything. A gap between users and non-users mixes the app's effect with that head start, and the data alone cannot pull them apart. To find out, let chance decide who gets the app, then compare the two groups.

What "the app helped" means

Take one student, Maya. The app helped her if she scored higher with it than she would have scored without it. That is a comparison of the same person in two versions of the same term. We can only ever see one of them: once Maya has used the app, her no-app score is gone. Researchers call the missing score the counterfactual, and its absence is the central difficulty of the whole subject.

Across a class, the question becomes "what would the average be if everyone used the app, versus if no one did?" A users-versus-non-users gap answers a different question. It compares two different sets of people in the real world. Hernán and Robins give an invented case where 54% of treated patients die against 43% of untreated ones, yet the death rate would be 50% whether everyone was treated or no one was. A gap like that arises when the sicker patients are the ones who get treated.

Who chose the app

Suppose six students take an exam, and the app adds exactly 2 points for everyone. The three most motivated students choose to use it.

Users average 80 and non-users average 62, an 18-point gap. Only 2 points come from the app. The other 16 are the difference between what users and non-users would have scored with no app at all, which is called selection bias. Cunningham shows the observed gap always splits this way, with one extra term when people gain unequal amounts. In his ten-patient version, a treatment that adds 0.6 years of life on average looks like it costs 0.4 years, because patients sorted themselves into whichever option suited them. So a gap can exaggerate a real benefit, hide one, or point the wrong way.

Causation can also run backward, since students doing well may enjoy the app more. And heavier users are often the most motivated, which is why federal evaluators warned that their own link between more use and higher scores has no causal meaning.

Why "controlling for" differences falls short

The usual fix is to compare users with non-users who match on measured traits, such as last year's grades and family income. That removes only the differences someone measured. Motivation that was never recorded stays in the gap, and Hernán and Robins point out that no analysis of the data can confirm that nothing important was missed.

LaLonde tested this directly. A job-training program had been run as a randomized experiment, so its true effect on 1978 earnings was known: about +$886. He replaced the experiment's control group with similar-looking people from national surveys and applied the standard adjustments.

Letting chance decide

If a coin flip decides who gets the app, motivated and unmotivated students land in both groups in roughly equal shares, including traits nobody measured. The no-app averages of the two groups then match, apart from luck, so selection bias disappears and the gap estimates the app's effect. In Hernán and Robins' words, "In ideal randomized experiments, association is causation."

Luck still matters in a small group, which is why trials use many students and report how large a gap chance alone could produce. Flip coins for the six students above, then for a class of 600 built from 100 copies of each, and compare how far single flips stray from the true 2 points.

What real trials found

Randomized trials of learning software have given both answers. In the U.S. federal study, teachers were randomly assigned to use one of 16 products or to teach as usual. Before the software was used, the groups matched on fall reading scores. By spring, first-grade differences were tiny.

The effect size divides each difference by the spread of scores; under 0.10 counts as small on the review's scale, and 0.03 is about 0 to 2 percentile points. The p-value is the chance of a gap at least this large if the software did nothing; 0.34 means luck explains it easily. Grades 4 and 6 showed the same pattern.

After four and a half months, winners led by 0.37 in math and 0.23 in Hindi in the published estimates, large by the same scale. The losers improved too, so a before-and-after look at winners alone would have overstated the effect. Across 29 trials of learning software in wealthy countries, the review counted 20 positive results, mostly in math, 8 with no effect, and 1 negative.

Where the answer stops

A trial answers a narrower question than it seems. Delhi winners' average attendance was only 58%, so comparing all winners with all losers measures the effect of offering the program, not of using it fully. Comparing heavy and light users within the trial brings self-selection back. Results also belong to one product, place, and test, and none of the trials described here studied a self-study phone app.

When chance cannot decide, researchers look for something close to it: an as-if-random event, a score cutoff for eligibility, or groups that were changing alike before the app arrived. Each design swaps the coin flip for an assumption that the data cannot fully confirm. So when someone reports that app users score higher, the question to ask is who was compared with whom, and how they ended up in their groups.
