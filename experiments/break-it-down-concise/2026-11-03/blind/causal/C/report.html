Causal inference from first principles.

Sources

  • Causal Inference: What If↗ Miguel A. Hernán and James M. Robins · Textbook, free online edition of the Chapman & Hall/CRC book · First online 2020; version read dated 19 August 2026

    A graduate textbook by two Harvard epidemiologists whose work built much of the modern mathematics of cause and effect. Its opening chapters define a causal effect as a comparison of the same people under two "what if" conditions, show with a small invented population how treated and untreated groups can differ when the treatment does nothing, and explain why adjusting non-experimental data depends on an assumption nobody can check. Its examples are medical and hypothetical, so they teach the logic and measure nothing about learning apps.

  • Causal Inference: The Mixtape↗ Scott Cunningham · Textbook, free online first edition · Yale University Press · 2021

    An economics textbook by a Baylor University economist, widely used to teach applied causal inference. Its potential-outcomes chapter works through ten invented patients whose outcomes are known both ways, which lets it show the exact arithmetic by which a helpful treatment can look harmful when people sort themselves into it. Because the patients are invented, the example shows how the distortion works, not how large it is for any real program.

  • Effectiveness of Reading and Mathematics Software Products: Findings from the First Student Cohort↗ Mark Dynarski, Roberto Agodini, and colleagues · Government evaluation report to Congress · U.S. Department of Education, Institute of Education Sciences · March 2007

    A national study ordered by Congress and run by two independent research firms, Mathematica and SRI. In 132 schools, teachers were randomly assigned to use one of 16 commercial reading or math programs or to teach as usual, and about 9,400 students were tested. It is a real randomized test of learning software whose answer, after one year, was no measurable gain. It covers particular products in their first year, used for roughly a tenth of class time, so it does not show that all learning software fails; a second-year follow-up found one product with a significant positive effect.

  • Disrupting Education? Experimental Evidence on Technology-Aided Instruction in India↗ Karthik Muralidharan, Abhijeet Singh, and Alejandro J. Ganimian · Peer-reviewed journal article · American Economic Review · April 2019

    Economists who run large school experiments in India studied an after-school program built around adaptive learning software. More students applied than there were seats, so a lottery chose who got free access, and winners scored well above losers after four and a half months. The text read was the 2017 working paper, whose numbers differ slightly from the published version; the published figures are used here. It covers 619 Delhi students in a program that combined the software with a tutor and extra time, so it shows that this program helped in that setting, not that any app will.

  • Evaluating the Econometric Evaluations of Training Programs with Experimental Data↗ Robert J. LaLonde · Peer-reviewed journal article · American Economic Review · September 1986

    A landmark paper that tested the standard statistical fixes. A job-training program had been run as a randomized experiment, so its true effect on earnings was known. LaLonde replaced the experiment's comparison group with people drawn from national surveys, applied the adjustments economists then relied on, and got answers that were often far off and sometimes of the wrong sign. It concerns job training rather than schooling, and later methods came closer on the same data, so it shows how wrong adjusted comparisons can be, not that they always fail.

  • Impact Evaluation in Practice, Second Edition↗ Paul J. Gertler, Sebastian Martinez, and colleagues · Practitioner textbook · World Bank and Inter-American Development Bank · 2016

    An openly licensed handbook by economists who evaluate government programs, used to train policy evaluators. It explains in plain terms why the two most tempting comparisons, the same people before and after and the people who chose not to join, mislead, and it runs one program through every major method. That program and its data are invented by the authors for teaching, so its numbers show how methods behave, not what any real program achieved.

  • Education Technology: An Evidence-Based Review↗ Maya Escueta, Vincent Quan, Andre Joshua Nickow, and Philip Oreopoulos · Review, working paper · National Bureau of Economic Research · August 2017

    A review by education economists, including researchers at the poverty-research center J-PAL, that included only randomized and similarly rigorous studies because, they write, much of the field rests on simple comparisons of users and non-users. It counts how many trials of learning software found gains. It is the 2017 version, later revised for the Journal of Economic Literature, and it tallies studies rather than averaging their effects.

The short answer

App users and non-users are different people. Students who choose a learning app may already be more motivated, better prepared, or better supported at home, and those same traits raise exam scores with or without the app. So the gap in average scores mixes whatever the app did with how the two groups would have differed anyway, and the data on their own cannot separate the two. To find out whether the app helped, you need a comparison group that would have scored the same as the users if neither had the app. The most reliable way to get one is to let chance decide who gets the app, by a coin flip or lottery, and then compare the average scores of the two groups.

What you actually observed

The observation "app users score higher" is a difference between two averages: the average exam score of students who used the app minus the average score of students who did not. That number is real, and it is useful for some purposes. If a school wants to predict which students will do well, knowing who uses the app helps. Statisticians call this kind of pattern an association, meaning two things tend to occur together in data, with nothing yet said about why.

"The app helped" is a different kind of statement. It says that a student who used the app scored higher than that same student would have scored without it. Hernán and Robins draw the distinction in one picture.

The two sides can disagree badly. In the book's invented population of 20 people, 7 of the 13 treated die (about 54%) against 3 of the 7 untreated (about 43%), so the treatment looks harmful. Yet in the full "what if" table, 10 of 20 would die if everyone were treated and 10 of 20 if no one were. The treatment does nothing. The authors explain the gap simply: it "would not be surprising if those who received heart transplants were, on average, sicker than those who did not." The same book describes an aspirin example where a drug that halves the risk of death looks as if it raises risk by half, because doctors give it to people already at high risk.

What "the app helped" means for one student

Take one student, Maya, who used the app and scored 82. To say the app helped Maya is to say that if she had not used it, she would have scored less than 82. That second score, the one from the world that did not happen, is called a counterfactual. Every student has two possible scores, one with the app and one without, and the app's effect on that student is the difference between them. Researchers call the pair potential outcomes.

The difficulty is that each student lives only one of those lives. Maya used the app, so her no-app score is never observed. A student who skipped the app has an observed no-app score and a missing app score. Every causal question has this missing half, and textbooks call it the fundamental problem of causal inference. Nothing in the data fills it in. Any method for estimating an effect is a way of supplying a believable stand-in for the missing scores.

From one student to the average effect

Individual effects can never be seen, but an average effect over many students can be estimated. The average causal effect of the app is the average score if every student used it minus the average score if no student did. Both averages are over the same students, so the only difference between them is the app.

The users-versus-non-users gap is an average over different students. Cunningham's textbook shows how far apart the two numbers can be with a table no real study could ever have, because it lists both outcomes for every person.

What only an all-knowing observer sees

What a researcher would actually record

Each patient got whichever treatment suited them best (D = 1 means surgery). Surgery patients average 7 years, chemotherapy patients 7.4, so the observed comparison says surgery costs 0.4 years.

The groups differed in what they would have experienced anyway, and that difference swamped the treatment. A gap between users and non-users can overstate a real benefit, hide one, or reverse its sign.

Why the gap and the effect differ: self-selection

When students choose whether to use the app, they self-select, and the gap between their averages splits into two parts:

observed gap = average effect of the app + selection bias

Selection bias here has a precise meaning you can compute with averages. It is what the app users would have scored without the app minus what the non-users actually scored without it. If motivated students both pick the app and study harder, users would have scored higher even with no app, and that head start lands in the gap. Cunningham's decomposition of his ten-patient example gives −0.4 years observed = 0.6 years of true effect − 4.8 years of selection bias + 3.8 years from patients gaining unequal amounts. The last term vanishes when every person gains the same amount, as in the example below. He argues that the whole enterprise of causal inference amounts to finding a believable way to neutralize selection bias.

A constructed example with six students

Suppose the app raises every student's score by exactly 2 points. Three motivated students, A, B, and C, choose the app; three others do not. These numbers are invented to show the arithmetic, not taken from any study. The users average 80 and the non-users 62, an 18-point gap. Of that, 2 points are the app. The other 16 are selection bias: without the app the users would have averaged 78, already 16 above the non-users' 62.

With 6 students there are only 20 possible ways to pick 3 users. Their gaps run from −14 to +18 points and average exactly 2.

Two things show up when you use the panel. Chance assignment is right on average: across many draws the gap centers on the true 2 points, because motivated and unmotivated students land on each side equally often. Any single small draw can still miss by a lot, and the miss shrinks as the class grows. Real trials therefore use many students and report how much chance alone could have moved the result.

Other ways to get a gap without the app helping

Self-selection is the main trap, but it has relatives.

  • The arrow can run from scores to app use, which is called reverse causation. Students who are already doing well may enjoy the app and keep using it, while students who are struggling may give up on it. The app then marks who was succeeding rather than producing the success.
  • Comparing users' scores before and after they started the app counts everything else that happened in between: a year of school, a better teacher, growing older. In the Delhi lottery study described below, students who lost the lottery and never got the program still improved in math by about a third of a standard deviation over the same months. A before-and-after look at the winners alone would have credited that ordinary growth to the program. The World Bank handbook warns that "the baseline outcome is almost never a good estimate of the counterfactual."
  • A pattern such as "students who used the app more scored higher" is again a comparison of students who chose different amounts of use. The federal evaluators found that in grade 4 reading "effects were larger when teachers reported higher levels of product use," and added: "these relationships do not have a causal interpretation." That warning applies inside a randomized study, because the amount of use was not randomized.

Why "controlling for" differences is not enough

A natural fix is to compare users with non-users who look similar: same prior grades, same family income, same school. Statistical tools such as regression and matching do this. They remove differences in the traits you measured, and only those. Motivation, a parent who checks homework, or a quiet place to study may never be recorded, and they can still differ between the groups. Hernán and Robins put the problem plainly: "the investigators cannot empirically verify" that no such hidden difference remains. The data cannot tell you what you failed to measure.

LaLonde's 1986 study shows what this means in practice. A job-training program had been tested by random assignment, so its true effect was known: trainees earned about $886 more in 1978 than the randomly chosen comparison group. LaLonde then threw away that comparison group, substituted people from national surveys, and applied the standard adjustments.

A small number attached to a result does not rescue it. The World Bank handbook's invented health-insurance example compares households that chose to enroll with those that did not and reports a difference of −$14.46 in health spending with a t-statistic of −49, which means the gap is far too large to be luck. It is still the wrong answer; the randomized comparison in the same example gives −$10.14. Statistical significance asks whether a gap could be chance. It says nothing about whether the gap was caused by the program.

How to find out: let chance choose

Random assignment attacks the missing-score problem directly. If a coin flip or lottery decides who gets the app, students' motivation, preparation, and home support have no say in which group they join. Each trait, measured or not, ends up in both groups in about equal shares. The non-users then become a fair stand-in for what the users would have scored without the app, and selection bias is zero on average. The simple difference in average scores becomes an estimate of the app's average effect. Hernán and Robins state the result in one line: "In ideal randomized experiments, association is causation."

This works even for traits nobody can measure, and that is what no amount of statistical adjustment can offer. The federal evaluators gave an example from their own study: a teacher's ability to motivate students cannot be observed, yet it was "'controlled' by the experimental design because teachers were randomly assigned."

Randomization balances groups on average, not exactly, so careful trials check. Before the software was used, the federal study compared the randomly assigned classrooms.

Reading the results of real trials

A randomized result has three parts worth reading: the difference in averages, how much chance alone could have produced, and how large the difference is in practical terms. Education studies usually report size as an effect size, the difference divided by the typical spread of scores (the standard deviation, or SD). On a bell-shaped test, 1 SD is roughly the distance from the 50th to the 84th percentile. The Escueta review offers a rule of thumb: under 0.10 SD is small, 0.10 to 0.25 encouraging, 0.25 to 0.40 large, and above 0.40 very large.

A follow-up the next year found one of ten products, a fourth-grade reading program, with a significant positive effect. The study tells you that being assigned these products, as schools actually used them, did not measurably raise scores that year.

The Delhi study found the opposite. A lottery gave 314 of 619 applicants free seats in an after-school program built around adaptive software.

Two randomized trials of learning software reached opposite answers, which is why the answer has to be measured product by product. The Escueta review, which counted only rigorous studies because it found the field "flooded with observational research," tallied 29 randomized trials of learning software in wealthy countries. Eight found no effect and one a negative effect; most of the rest found gains, often in math.

Wrinkles in real experiments

Random assignment makes the comparison fair, but real trials still have to decide what exactly they are estimating.

  • Being offered the app is not the same as using it. In Delhi, lottery winners attended on average only 58% of the time. Comparing everyone offered a seat with everyone not offered keeps the comparison fair and measures the effect of the offer. Comparing heavy attenders with light ones would bring self-selection back. The researchers estimated the effect of actual attendance by scaling the offer's effect by how much it raised attendance, which rests on extra assumptions.
  • Some students drop out before the final test. In Delhi, 84% of winners and 88% of losers took the final test. If the students who disappear differ between groups, the groups are no longer balanced, so the researchers checked how much this could move the answer.
  • Effects can spill over. If app users share what they learn with friends in the comparison group, the comparison group is no longer a clean picture of life without the app.
  • Results belong to their setting. A trial measures one product, used a certain way, by certain students, on a certain test. The federal evaluators wrote that their findings "do not support conclusions about technology's effectiveness beyond the study's context."

When you cannot flip a coin

Sometimes randomizing is impossible or unethical. Researchers then look for a situation that comes close, and each such design trades the coin flip for an assumption that cannot be fully checked.

  • In a natural experiment, something outside the researchers' control already sorts people as if at random. When an oversubscribed program hands out places by lottery, as in Delhi, the lottery does the randomizing.
  • Regression discontinuity uses an eligibility cutoff. If a school gives the app only to students who scored below 60 on a placement test, students who scored 59 and 61 are nearly alike, so comparing them approximates a coin flip. This assumes nothing else changes at the cutoff. The answer applies to students near the cutoff.
  • Difference-in-differences compares how much users' scores changed over a year with how much non-users' scores changed. This assumes both groups would have improved by the same amount without the app.
  • Matching pairs each user with a non-user who looks similar on measured traits. It assumes no unmeasured difference remains, the same assumption LaLonde's table shows can fail.

What this means when you read a claim

When someone says app users score higher, the useful question is "compared with whom, and how were they chosen?" If the users chose themselves, the gap tells you about the users as much as the app, and you cannot tell how much of each. That cuts both ways: the gap does not prove the app helped, and it does not prove the app failed either, since self-selection can also hide a real benefit. If chance chose, the gap is a fair estimate of the app's average effect for those students, that product, and that test, within a margin set by how many students took part.

The evidence here has limits that matter for the app question. Neither of the two randomized trials tested a consumer smartphone app that students use alone; one covered classroom software and the other an after-school program with tutors. LaLonde's warning about adjusted comparisons comes from job training, not schooling. The textbook examples are invented to show the mechanism. What carries over to any learning app is the logic: a fair comparison group is what turns a difference in averages into a measured effect.
