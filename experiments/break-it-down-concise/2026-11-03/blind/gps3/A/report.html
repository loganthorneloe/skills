The short answer

Your phone only listens. Each GPS satellite broadcasts where it is and what time its clock reads. The phone times how long each signal took to arrive, and since radio travels at the speed of light, each delay gives a distance. Distances to four satellites fix the phone's position and also the error in its own cheap clock. The satellites' clocks have no such fix, and light covers 30 cm in a billionth of a second, so they must be atomic clocks accurate to nanoseconds and corrected even for relativity.

What the satellites send

About 31 GPS satellites (as of July 2023) circle the Earth twice a day at about 20,200 km, spread over six tilted orbits so that at least four are visible "from virtually any point on the planet," according to GPS.gov.

Each satellite sends a repeating pattern of bits unique to it, called a pseudo-random noise (PRN) code, which on the civil signal repeats every millisecond. On top of it rides the navigation message, a 50 bit-per-second stream that the GPS specification says "includes SV ephemerides, system time, SV clock behavior data". SV means satellite. The ephemeris is a set of orbit parameters giving the satellite's position at any moment, and the clock data says how far its clock is from GPS time. Together they tell the phone where each signal came from and when it left.

Time becomes distance

The phone keeps its own copy of each PRN code and slides it until it lines up with the incoming signal. The slide is the delay, and delay times the speed of light is the pseudorange:

ρ = (tRx − tTx) · c

tTx is the satellite's transmit time stamp, tRx the phone's receive time, and c is 299,792,458 m/s. For a satellite 20,200 km overhead, the delay is about 67 milliseconds. Every nanosecond of clock error shifts the answer by about 0.3 m; Ashby puts it as "Timing errors of one ns will lead to positioning errors of the order of 30 cm." It is a pseudorange because it equals the true distance only if the two clocks agree, and the phone's never does.

Spheres, and the fourth unknown

One distance puts the phone somewhere on a sphere around the satellite. Two spheres meet in a circle, and a third cuts that circle at two points, one of them far out in space.

Real phones use quartz clocks. If one reads 1 millisecond fast, every pseudorange comes out about 300 km too long, by the same amount for every satellite, because all are timed against the same clock. As the European GNSS Agency's paper notes, "the three spheres do not intersect at a common point." So the phone treats its clock error b as an unknown alongside its position (x, y, z). For each satellite i at its known position:

ρi = √[(x − xi)² + (y − yi)² + (z − zi)²] + c·b

Four unknowns need four equations, which is why GPS needs four satellites, not three. Because of the square root, receivers solve by starting from a guess, linearizing, and repeating until the answer settles. With more than four satellites, a least-squares fit uses all of them and averages down each one's noise. Solving for b also sets the phone's clock, which is why GPS doubles as a time service.

Find the clock error that makes the circles meet

A flat, two-dimensional version of the problem. In two dimensions there are three unknowns (x, y and the clock error), so three satellites are enough; in three dimensions the same logic needs four.

Every circle is 12.0 km too large (40 µs × 0.2998 km/µs), so the crossing points spread over 8.3 km instead of meeting at one point.

The geometry is simplified. Satellites are placed about 110 km away instead of 20,200 km so the circles fit, and the ranges have no noise. The one real relationship is the conversion 1 µs of clock error = 0.2998 km of range error. Without JavaScript the picture shows the default state: a 40 µs error with no correction.

One number fixes every circle at once, which is what makes the clock error solvable: it adds the same length to every range, while moving the position changes each range differently.

Why the satellite clocks must be so precise

The phone cannot solve for errors in a satellite's clock, because that satellite's time stamp is the reference. If it is 10 ns off, that range is 3 m wrong and nothing in the equations reveals it. So the satellites carry atomic clocks, which count vibrations of cesium or rubidium atoms.

Quartz is enough for the phone because it re-solves its clock error with every fix. Ground stations monitor the satellite clocks and upload the small corrections carried in the navigation message.

Relativity is large enough to matter

At nanosecond precision, Einstein matters. A moving clock runs slow (special relativity), and a clock in weaker gravity runs fast (general relativity). At GPS altitude, gravity wins.

The specification gives the net offset as "Δf/f = -4.4647E-10". Over 86,400 seconds that is about 38.6 microseconds a day, or 11.6 km of light travel; Ashby writes that uncorrected, a single day's drift "would cause navigational errors of more than 11 km." So each clock is set before launch to 10.2299999954326 MHz, so it ticks at exactly 10.23 MHz as seen from the ground. This was measured, not assumed: the first orbiting cesium clock, on NTS-2 in 1977, ran fast by 442.5 parts in 1012 against a predicted 446.5. A small leftover term from the slightly elliptical orbits is applied by the receiver.

What still limits accuracy

With the clocks handled, the main errors come from the signal's path and the phone's surroundings.

The ionosphere and lower atmosphere delay the signal; receivers subtract a model, and two-frequency receivers can measure the ionospheric part. Buildings block signals or reflect them into longer paths, called multipath. Geometry matters too: satellites spread across the sky fix a position better than bunched ones.

The government commits only to the signal: a global average range error of at most 2.0 m, 95% of the time (about 0.64 m on one reported day in 2021). GPS.gov says smartphones are typically accurate to within 4.9 m under open sky, and worse near buildings, bridges, and trees.

How your phone gets a fix in seconds

At 50 bits per second, one 1,500-bit message frame takes 30 seconds, so decoding each satellite's orbit and clock data from the sky is slow. Phones use assisted GNSS instead: they download that data, the time, a rough position from cell towers, and an ionosphere model over the network. The white paper states, "The main aim of A-GNSS is to reduce TTFF," the time to first fix.

Phones also use Galileo, GLONASS, and BeiDou, which work the same way. More satellites improve geometry, though each system adds one unknown, its clock offset from GPS time.

Limits

The satellite count is a 2023 figure, the error budget is generic, and the 4.9 m figure comes from open-sky tests that city streets will not match. Phones also blend GPS with Wi-Fi, cell towers, and motion sensors, so the dot on your map is not always a pure satellite fix.
