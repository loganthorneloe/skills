### Causal Inference: What If
- url: https://miguelhernan.org/whatifbook ; text read from: author-hosted PDF https://miguelhernan.org/s/hernanrobins_WhatIf_19aug26.pdf (version dated August 19, 2026)
- byline: Miguel A. Hernán and James M. Robins · textbook (living online edition; print by Chapman & Hall/CRC) · self-hosted, citation "Boca Raton: Chapman & Hall/CRC" · first online 2020, revision 19 Aug 2026
- verdicts: C1 pass; C2 pass; C3 pass; C4 pass; C5 fail (not addressed in sections read); C6 pass; C7 pass; C8 pass; C9 fail (no ed-tech content); C10 conditional (only lists the "ideal randomized experiment" assumptions it sets aside: no loss to follow-up, full adherence, single version of treatment, blinding; does not treat them in sections read); C11 conditional (only names conditional randomization and instrumental variables as alternative routes in Ch. 3; methods detail is in later chapters not read)
- use: Ch. 1 §1.1 (individual causal effect, Zeus/Hera), §1.2 (average causal effect, Table 1.1), §1.4 (random variability), §1.5 (causation vs. association, Figure 1.1, the 7/13 vs 3/7 association with no causal effect, aspirin margin note); Ch. 2 intro and §2.1 (randomization, exchangeability, "In ideal randomized experiments, association is causation"); Ch. 3 intro, §3.1, §3.2 (observational studies, conditional exchangeability, unmeasured confounders, unverifiability)
- credibility: Hernán is Kolokotrones Professor and director of CAUSALab at the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health (verified on his own site); Robins originated much of the modern counterfactual methodology used in epidemiology. The book is the standard graduate text in epidemiological causal inference and has been revised continuously since 2020.
- contribution: Defines causal effects as contrasts of counterfactual outcomes in the same population and association as contrasts between the treated and untreated subsets; shows with a 20-person table that association can exist with zero causal effect; explains that randomization makes missing counterfactuals missing "by chance," yielding exchangeability; explains why observational adjustment depends on an unverifiable assumption that no unmeasured outcome predictor differs between groups.
- limits: Examples are medical (heart transplants, aspirin), stylized and hypothetical; notation-heavy for this reader (writers should translate, not quote formulas). Authors state that this stage of the online book may be revised without documenting changes; page numbers refer to the 19 Aug 2026 PDF. Rights: "All rights reserved"; reuse beyond U.S. copyright exceptions needs permission. No errata or retraction applies (textbook; author site states it is revised in place).
- read: partial (Ch. 1 full; Ch. 2 intro and §2.1 full; Ch. 3 intro, §3.1, §3.2 through the unmeasured-confounder discussion; remaining 300+ pages not read) · version 19aug26 · via author-hosted PDF

**Reader-facing entry draft.** A free graduate textbook by two Harvard epidemiologists who helped build the modern math of cause and effect. Its first chapters define a causal effect as a comparison of the same people in two "what if" worlds and show, with a small made-up example, how a real difference between treated and untreated groups can appear even when the treatment does nothing. It is the source here for the core definitions, for why randomization works, and for why adjusting observational data rests on an assumption no one can check. Its examples are medical and hypothetical, so they illustrate logic rather than measure anything about learning apps.

### Causal Inference: The Mixtape (Chapter 4, Potential Outcomes Causal Model; Chapter 5 §5.3.4, NSW job training example)
- url: https://mixtape-1ed.netlify.app/04-potential_outcomes and https://mixtape-1ed.netlify.app/05-Matching_and_Subclassification.html ; text read from: same (free online first edition). The current site mixtape.scunning.com now hosts a revised edition ("The Remix") whose chapter URLs differ; the 1st-edition pages were read.
- byline: Scott Cunningham · textbook (free online edition of the print book) · Yale University Press · 2021 (print); online 1st edition undated, accessed 2026-11-03
- verdicts: C1 pass; C2 pass; C3 pass; C4 pass; C5 conditional (reverse causation raised only as a critique of Yule's 1899 poverty regression, not in an education setting); C6 pass (via LaLonde 1986 account, spot-checked against the primary); C7 pass; C8 conditional (Monte Carlo illustration and randomization inference, not a lay account of significance); C9 fail; C10 conditional (SUTVA: no spillovers, same dose); C11 fail (later chapters not read)
- use: §4.0 (Yule regression critique), §4.1.1–4.1.4 (switching equation, fundamental problem, ATE/ATT/ATU, Tables 4.2–4.3 "perfect doctor" example, decomposition SDO = ATE + selection bias + heterogeneous-effect bias with −0.4 = 0.6 − 4.8 + 3.8, independence assumption, Monte Carlo), §4.1.5 (SUTVA); §5.3.4 (LaLonde 1986 Table 5.11 values, selection-bias explanation)
- credibility: Cunningham is the Ben H. Williams Professor of Economics at Baylor University (verified on his site); the book is published by Yale University Press and is widely used to teach applied causal inference to economics students.
- contribution: Gives the cleanest fully worked numeric example of why a comparison of chosen groups misleads: with all potential outcomes known, surgery helps on average (+0.6 years) yet the observed comparison says it hurts (−0.4), and the book shows exactly how selection bias (−4.8) and unequal gains (+3.8) produce that. Shows that random assignment zeroes out both bias terms. Recounts LaLonde's demonstration that standard adjusted observational estimates missed an experimental benchmark, often with the wrong sign.
- limits: The example is medical and invented (ten hypothetical patients). The heterogeneous-effect bias term only matters when the target is the population average effect; Cunningham notes Angrist and Pischke's version omits it. Internal inconsistency: text says the Monte Carlo "runs 10,000 times" while the shown Python code loops 1,000 times; the reported average SDO of 0.59088 is from the author's run. The online page has no stated reuse license (rights unstated); tables are HTML, so exhibit images were rendered from the page's own HTML and CSS. The 1st-edition site may be superseded by the Remix edition; the definitions used here are unchanged textbook material.
- read: partial (Ch. 4 §4.0–4.1.5 full, randomization-inference sections skimmed; Ch. 5 only §5.3.4) · 1st online edition · via mixtape-1ed.netlify.app

**Reader-facing entry draft.** An economics textbook from Yale University Press by a Baylor economist, free online. Its potential-outcomes chapter runs a ten-patient example in which we, unlike any real researcher, know every patient's outcome both ways. That lets it show the exact arithmetic by which a treatment that helps on average can look harmful when people sort themselves into it, and how random assignment removes that distortion. It also retells a famous test in which standard statistical adjustments failed to recover a known experimental answer. The patients are invented, so the example teaches the mechanism; it says nothing about how large selection bias is for any real app.

### Effectiveness of Reading and Mathematics Software Products: Findings from the First Student Cohort
- url: https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/resource-library/report/evaluation-report/effectiveness-reading-and-mathematics-software-products-findings-first-student-cohort ; text read from: IES PDFs https://ies.ed.gov/sites/default/files/migrated/nces_pubs/ncee/pdf/20074005.pdf (full report, NCEE 2007-4005) and .../20074006.pdf (executive summary, NCEE 2007-4006)
- byline: Mark Dynarski, Roberto Agodini, and colleagues (Mathematica Policy Research and SRI International) · government evaluation report to Congress · U.S. Department of Education, Institute of Education Sciences, National Center for Education Evaluation and Regional Assistance · March 2007
- verdicts: C1 fail; C2 fail; C3 fail; C4 conditional (states that random assignment controls unobservables such as a teacher's ability to motivate students; does not itself measure selection bias); C5 pass (states that correlations between effects and usage or school characteristics "cannot be interpreted as causal"); C6 fail; C7 pass; C8 pass (Table II.5 balance with p-values; one chance imbalance in master's degrees; p-values for effects; sampling variance explaining school differences); C9 pass (for the products, grades, and first year studied); C10 pass (real-world conditions: usage about 10–11% of instructional time, teachers could stop using products, control teachers could use other software, volunteer districts, non-representative products, results not generalizable beyond context); C11 fail
- use: Executive summary pp. iii–xiv (design, findings, usage, Figures 1–5); full report Ch. II pp. 27–29 (Table II.5 baseline characteristics, Table II.6 first-grade score differences, effect-size definition footnote 15)
- credibility: Congressionally mandated national evaluation designed, run, and analyzed by two established independent research organizations (Mathematica, SRI) under federal contract, with 33 districts, 132 schools, 439 teachers and 9,424 tested students; teachers were randomly assigned within schools; analysis used hierarchical models and robustness checks with district tests. In the public domain.
- contribution: Shows a real randomized test of commercial learning software: classrooms randomly assigned to use one of 16 selected reading or math products did not score significantly higher after one school year (first-grade effect sizes −0.01 to 0.06, about 0 to 2 percentile points). Shows baseline balance from randomization and how an RCT reports uncertainty.
- limits: Products were volunteered by vendors and chosen partly for having prior evidence of effectiveness; not representative of all software. Results are for groups of products, not individual products, and for the first year with teachers new to them. Usage was modest (roughly 10% of instructional time), so the result is the effect of being assigned to use the products as schools actually did, not of heavy use. Software-industry representatives disputed the findings (EdWeek, April 2007, seen in search results only, not read). The follow-up two-cohort report (Campuzano et al., February 2009, NCEE 2009-4041) found second-year impacts for 1st and 4th grade reading unchanged, 6th-grade math impacts worsened, algebra impacts improved, and only one of 10 individual products (LeapTrack, 4th-grade reading) with a statistically significant positive effect (verified on the IES evaluation page; follow-up report itself not read). Escueta et al. (2017) note the multi-product aggregation makes these results hard to compare with single-product studies. Old (2004–05 school year); software has changed, so the numbers are historical, while the methodological lesson is current.
- read: partial (executive summary full; full report Ch. II pages 27–30 and table list; other chapters and appendices not read) · NCEE 2007-4005/4006 · via IES PDF

**Reader-facing entry draft.** A national study Congress ordered from the U.S. Department of Education, carried out by two independent research firms. In 132 schools, teachers were randomly assigned either to use one of 16 commercial reading or math programs or to teach as usual, and about 9,400 students were tested in fall and spring. After one year, classrooms using the software did not score measurably higher. It shows what a real randomized test of learning software looks like and that its answer can be "no detectable gain." It covers specific products in their first year, used about a tenth of class time, in mostly high-poverty schools, so it does not say that all learning software fails; a second-year follow-up found one product with a significant positive effect.

### Disrupting Education? Experimental Evidence on Technology-Aided Instruction in India
- url: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20171112 (published) ; text read from: NBER Working Paper 22923, revised July 2017 https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22923/w22923.pdf ; published abstract read on the AEA page
- byline: Karthik Muralidharan, Abhijeet Singh, and Alejandro J. Ganimian · peer-reviewed journal article (working paper version read) · American Economic Review 109(4): 1426–60 · April 2019 (working paper December 2016, revised July 2017)
- verdicts: C1 fail; C2 fail; C3 fail; C4 conditional (states attendance "may be endogenous to expected gains," which is why they use the lottery); C5 pass (dose-response among lottery winners treated as needing the lottery instrument, not read off the raw correlation); C6 fail; C7 pass; C8 pass (baseline balance, 95% confidence intervals in Figure 2); C9 pass (for this program and setting); C10 pass (intent-to-treat vs. effect of attendance via IV, 58% mean attendance, differential follow-up 84% vs 88% with inverse-probability weighting and Lee bounds, composite program, school-test vs own-test differences); C11 conditional (illustrates instrumental variables using a randomized offer; not a general account)
- use: Abstract; Introduction pp. 1–5; §2.2–2.3 (sample, randomization, compliance, attrition); §4.4 (grade-level tests); §4.5 (IV dose-response); §4.6.1 (attrition robustness); Figure 2 (p. 31)
- credibility: Muralidharan (UC San Diego) is a leading development economist who runs large education experiments; the study has an AEA RCT registry entry (trial 980; registration timing not checked), funded by J-PAL, published in the American Economic Review after peer review, with replication data deposited on openICPSR. Mindspark's developer (Educational Initiatives) operated the centers; the authors ran independent paper tests.
- contribution: A lottery randomly gave 314 of 619 Delhi middle-school applicants free access to an after-school program built around adaptive software. Lottery winners scored 0.37 SD higher in math and 0.23 SD higher in Hindi after 4.5 months (published figures; working paper: 0.36 and 0.22). Instrumental-variable estimates scale this to about 0.6 SD (math) and 0.39 SD (Hindi) for 90 days of attendance (working paper: 0.59 and 0.36).
- limits: Small sample (619), self-selected applicants in three centers in Delhi; program combines software, a teaching assistant, and extra time, so the software's separate effect is not experimentally isolated; effects in math showed up only on below-grade-level items and not on school exams; follow-up rates differed (84% vs 88%); IV scaling assumes constant effects and a linear dose-response. Numbers differ slightly between the 2017 working paper and the 2019 published version; cite the published abstract figures. No erratum or failed replication found in one targeted search; a larger in-school Mindspark study is ongoing (not read). Rights: NBER notice allows quoting short sections of text with credit; figure reuse rights unstated.
- read: partial (abstract, introduction, §2.2–2.3, §4.4–4.6.1, figure notes pp. 31–33; other sections, tables, appendices not read) · NBER WP 22923 revised July 2017 + AER 2019 abstract · via NBER PDF

**Reader-facing entry draft.** A peer-reviewed study in the American Economic Review by economists who run large school experiments in India. Because more students wanted a seat in an after-school program built around adaptive learning software than there were seats, a lottery chose who got free access, which made winners and losers comparable. After about four and a half months, winners scored well above losers in math and Hindi. It shows a randomized comparison detecting a large effect, and how researchers handle students who won a seat but rarely came. It covers 619 Delhi students in a program that paired the software with a tutor and extra time, so it shows that well-designed technology can help in that setting, not that any app will.

### Evaluating the Econometric Evaluations of Training Programs with Experimental Data
- url: https://www.jstor.org/stable/1806062 ; text read from: scanned copy of the published article at https://gwern.net/doc/economics/1986-lalonde.pdf (JSTOR scan, 17 pages)
- byline: Robert J. LaLonde · peer-reviewed journal article · American Economic Review 76(4): 604–620 · September 1986
- verdicts: C6 pass; C7 conditional (uses the randomized experiment as the benchmark; does not itself explain randomization for lay readers); all other claims fail (not addressed)
- use: Abstract and introduction (p. 604); Table 5 and its notes (p. 610)
- credibility: Primary research by a labor economist (then University of Chicago) using public data from the National Supported Work Demonstration, a randomized job-training experiment; one of the most cited methodological papers in economics and the origin of a long line of "within-study comparison" work (see Dehejia and Wahba 1999 as discussed in the Mixtape).
- contribution: Takes a program whose true effect is known from random assignment (about +$886 in 1978 earnings for male trainees, standard error $476) and replaces the experimental control group with comparison groups drawn from national surveys, then applies the adjustment methods economists used. Many estimates are far off and of the wrong sign (for example −$15,578 unadjusted and −$8,067 adjusted with the PSID-1 comparison group).
- limits: Job training and earnings, not education; methods from the early 1980s; later work (Dehejia and Wahba 1999, 2002) showed that propensity-score methods on a subsample came closer to the benchmark, and the lesson is that results depend heavily on comparison group and method, not that adjustment always fails. Read from a scanned mirror; OCR used only for location, values checked visually on the rendered page. Rights: AEA/JSTOR copyright ("Copyright © 2001 All Rights Reserved" on the scan); reuse of the table image rights unstated for this use.
- read: partial (abstract, introduction p. 604, Table 5 page p. 610; rest skimmed for the $886 statement p. 616) · published version · via gwern.net mirror of the JSTOR scan

**Reader-facing entry draft.** A 1986 American Economic Review paper that became a landmark because it tested the tests. A job-training program had been run as a randomized experiment, so its real effect on earnings was known. LaLonde then threw away the experiment's control group, substituted comparison groups from national surveys, and applied the statistical adjustments economists of the day relied on. Many answers were far off, and several said the program cut earnings when it raised them. It concerns job training rather than schooling, and later methods did better on the same data, so it shows how wrong adjusted comparisons can be, not that they always fail.

### Impact Evaluation in Practice, Second Edition
- url: https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/handle/10986/25030 ; text read from: World Bank Open Knowledge Repository PDF (English, 367 pp.)
- byline: Paul J. Gertler, Sebastian Martinez, Patrick Premand, Laura B. Rawlings, and Christel M. J. Vermeersch · practitioner textbook · World Bank and Inter-American Development Bank · 2016
- verdicts: C1 pass; C2 pass; C3 conditional (framed as "counterfactual" for a group; no explicit whole-population contrast); C4 pass; C5 pass (before-and-after "counterfeit" estimate); C6 conditional (shows adjustment narrowing but not closing the gap in a fictional dataset; not real-world evidence); C7 pass; C8 conditional (t-statistics and standard errors in tables; balance table; sample-size chapter not read); C9 fail; C10 conditional (valid comparison group must be unaffected by the program, i.e., no spillovers; Ch. 9 on noncompliance not read); C11 pass (Table 11.2 summarizes randomized assignment, IV, RDD, DiD, matching with key assumptions)
- use: Ch. 3 (pp. 47–61: causal inference, counterfactual, "perfect clone" Figure 3.1, valid comparison group's three conditions, two "counterfeit" counterfactuals, Tables 3.1–3.4); Ch. 4 Tables 4.1–4.3 (pp. 83–84); Tables 5.2, 6.1, 7.2–7.3, 8.2 (HISP estimates by method); Table 11.2 (pp. 194–195)
- credibility: Written by World Bank and academic evaluation specialists (Gertler is a UC Berkeley economist (unverified)) who run impact evaluations of government programs; a widely used training text for policy evaluators, published openly under CC BY 3.0 IGO.
- contribution: Explains in plain language why a program's impact needs a counterfactual, why "before vs. after" and "enrolled vs. not enrolled" comparisons are "counterfeit," and what a valid comparison group must satisfy. Its running case (a health-insurance subsidy, HISP) applies each method to the same data: before-after −$6.65; enrolled vs. non-enrolled −$14.46 (−$9.98 with controls); randomized assignment −$10.14 (−$10.01 with controls); randomized promotion (IV) −$9.50/−$9.74; regression discontinuity −$9.03; difference-in-differences −$8.16; matching −$9.95 (full covariates) or −$11.35 (limited).
- limits: HISP is explicitly a "fictional case study" with a constructed dataset; its numbers illustrate the methods and cannot be cited as evidence about any real program. The randomized result is the book's implied benchmark only by construction. Table 4.1 note defines only "**" while two entries carry "*" (minor inconsistency). Health spending, not education. Rights: CC BY 3.0 IGO (attribution required).
- read: partial (Ch. 3 full; Ch. 4 tables and surrounding text; HISP result tables in Ch. 5–8 only; Table 11.2; other chapters not read) · 2nd edition 2016 · via World Bank OKR PDF

**Reader-facing entry draft.** An openly licensed handbook from the World Bank, written by economists who evaluate government programs for a living and used to train policy evaluators. Its third chapter explains why every impact estimate needs a stand-in for "what would have happened otherwise," and why the two most tempting stand-ins (the same people before the program, and the people who chose not to join) are "counterfeit." It then runs one made-up health-insurance program through every major method, so readers can watch the estimates diverge. The case and its data are fictional, so its numbers show how methods behave, not what any real program achieved.

### Education Technology: An Evidence-Based Review
- url: https://www.nber.org/papers/w23744 ; text read from: NBER PDF https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w23744/w23744.pdf
- byline: Maya Escueta, Vincent Quan, Andre Joshua Nickow, and Philip Oreopoulos · review paper (working paper) · NBER Working Paper 23744 · August 2017 (later published in revised form as "Upgrading Education with Technology: Insights from Experimental Research," Journal of Economic Literature 58(4), 2020; published version not read)
- verdicts: C7 conditional (summarizes the standard rationale for RCTs, citing others); C9 conditional (counts for developed-country CAL RCTs in the 2017 working paper; published counts may differ); C11 conditional (brief description of quasi-experiments and RDD); all other claims fail
- use: §2 Literature Review Methodology (pp. 5–8); §4.1 Computer-Assisted Learning overview (pp. 21–23) incl. footnotes 58–59
- credibility: Authors include J-PAL North America staff and Philip Oreopoulos, a University of Toronto economist who runs education field experiments; NBER working papers are not peer reviewed, but this one was later published in the Journal of Economic Literature, the AEA's review journal.
- contribution: Restricts its review to RCTs and regression discontinuity studies because, in its words, the ed-tech policy literature is "flooded with observational research." Of 29 developed-country RCTs of computer-assisted learning, it counts 8 with no effect and 1 negative, with most positive findings in math, and flags the IES 2007/2009 studies as notable null exceptions whose multi-product design makes comparison difficult.
- limits: Working-paper version (2017); the 2020 JEL version may have updated study counts; I did not read it. Counts studies rather than pooling effect sizes (vote counting), which says nothing about average size; focus on developed countries. Rights: NBER notice permits short text quotes with credit.
- read: partial (abstract, §1 end, §2, §4.1 and opening of §4.2; remaining sections not read) · NBER WP 23744 August 2017 · via NBER PDF

**Reader-facing entry draft.** A review by education economists, including researchers at the MIT-based poverty-research center J-PAL, that gathered only randomized and similarly rigorous studies of education technology, precisely because so much of the field rests on simple comparisons of users and non-users. Among 29 randomized trials of learning software in wealthy countries, most found gains, especially in math, while some, including the large federal study above, found none. It is a 2017 working paper, later revised for the Journal of Economic Literature, and it counts studies rather than averaging their effects.

## Gaps

- C5: only conditional sources for reverse causation specifically — Mixtape raises it for Yule's poverty regression; no education source read states reverse causation for app use. Before-after and usage-correlation parts of C5 pass (World Bank, IES, Mindspark). Reverse causation itself is settled textbook reasoning and may be stated as background.
- C6: real-world evidence is from job training (LaLonde 1986), not education; no within-study comparison of an education app (observational vs. experimental estimates in the same population) was found or read. The World Bank demonstration is fictional data.
- C8: no source read gives a lay explanation of p-values or statistical power; sources use them (IES, Mindspark, World Bank). Treat as settled background; do not cite a power calculation.
- C9: no randomized evaluation of a consumer self-study smartphone app (e.g., language-learning apps) was audited; evidence is from classroom software (IES) and an after-school blended program (Mindspark). Do not generalize to "learning apps" as a class beyond what these show.
- C10: noncompliance and attrition are supported by Mindspark and IES; spillovers only by definition (Mixtape SUTVA, World Bank condition 2).
- C11: only overview-level sources (World Bank Table 11.2, Escueta et al. on RDD, Hernán Ch. 3); method chapters for IV, RDD, DiD were not read in full.
- Disputed premise: none. The question's premise (a users-vs-non-users score gap does not by itself prove the app helped) is supported by every relevant source. Note that it also does not prove the app did not help; the gap could hide a real effect, a zero effect, or a harmful one (Mixtape's −0.4 vs +0.6 example shows sign reversal).
