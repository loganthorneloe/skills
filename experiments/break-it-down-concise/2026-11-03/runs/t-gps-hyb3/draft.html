The short answer

Your phone never sends anything to GPS satellites. Each satellite continuously broadcasts where it is and exactly what time its clock reads. Your phone notes when each broadcast arrives, and because radio travels at the speed of light, the delay tells it how far away each satellite is. Distances to several satellites at known positions pin down one spot on Earth. The catch is that light covers about 30 cm in a billionth of a second, so a timing error of even a millionth of a second becomes a 300 m position error. The satellites therefore carry atomic clocks, corrected even for Einstein's relativity, while the phone's cheap clock is handled by treating its error as one more unknown and solving for it with a fourth satellite.

What the satellites send

GPS satellites fly about 20,200 km up and circle the Earth twice a day. They are spread over six tilted orbits so that, as GPS.gov puts it, users can see at least four satellites "from virtually any point on the planet." The United States commits to at least 24 working satellites and has flown about 31 for over a decade (31 as of July 2023).

Each satellite transmits two things on top of each other. The first is a repeating pattern of ones and zeros called a pseudo-random noise (PRN) code. On the civil signal it is 1,023 bits long and repeats every millisecond, and each satellite has its own pattern, so a receiver can tell them apart. The second is a slow data stream, the navigation message, sent at only 50 bits per second. The specification lists its contents:

"The legacy navigation (LNAV) data, D(t), includes SV ephemerides, system time, SV clock behavior data, status messages..."
IS-GPS-200N, §3.2.2

"SV" means space vehicle, the satellite. The ephemeris is a set of orbit parameters that lets a receiver calculate where the satellite was at any moment. The clock data tells the receiver how far that satellite's clock is from official GPS time. Together, they give the receiver the two facts it needs from each satellite: where it was and when it sent the signal.

Time becomes distance

The receiver keeps its own copy of each satellite's PRN code and slides it in time until it lines up with the incoming signal. The amount it had to slide is the signal's delay. Multiplying that delay by the speed of light gives a distance, which GNSS engineers call a pseudorange:

ρ = (tRx − tTx) · c

Here tTx is the transmit time stamped by the satellite, tRx is the receive time read from the phone's clock, and c is 299,792,458 m/s. It is called a pseudorange because it is only the true distance if both clocks agree exactly, and the phone's never does.

For example, suppose a satellite is straight overhead, 20,200 km away. Its signal takes 20,200 km ÷ 299,792 km/s ≈ 0.0674 s, about 67 milliseconds, to arrive. The European GNSS Agency's paper gives the same order, a delay "of about 65 ms". That number is easy to measure. The hard part is that every nanosecond (billionth of a second) of error in either clock shifts the answer by c × 1 ns ≈ 0.3 m. Ashby states it directly: "Timing errors of one ns will lead to positioning errors of the order of 30 cm." The rest of this explanation follows this one example: a phone 20,200 km below a satellite whose clock may be off by some amount.

Spheres, and the fourth unknown

One distance tells the phone only that it lies somewhere on a sphere centered on the satellite with that radius. A second satellite's sphere cuts the first in a circle. A third sphere cuts that circle at two points, and one of them is far out in space, so the other is the answer.

That picture assumes the phone's clock is perfect. It is not. A phone uses a quartz crystal oscillator, and if its clock reads 1 millisecond fast, every measured delay is 1 ms too long and every pseudorange is about 300 km too long. The error is the same for every satellite because all the signals are timed against the same phone clock. The same white paper explains the effect: "Due to the impact of the receiver clock, the three spheres do not intersect at a common point."

The fix is to admit you do not know the clock error and solve for it. Call the phone's position (x, y, z) and its clock error b. For satellite i at the known position (xi, yi, zi), the measured pseudorange ρi is the true distance plus the distance light travels during the clock error:

ρi = √[(x − xi)² + (y − yi)² + (z − zi)²] + c·b

That is four unknowns, x, y, z and b, so the phone needs four equations, one per satellite. This is the real reason GPS needs four satellites, not three. The equations are not linear because of the square root, so receivers start from a rough guess, linearize around it, solve, and repeat until the answer stops changing. With more than four satellites, which is normal, the receiver uses least squares to find the position and clock error that best fit all the measurements together, which averages down the noise in each one.

A useful side effect is that once b is known, the phone's clock is corrected too. GPS is also a time service: the government specifies GPS time transfer to within 30 nanoseconds of official U.S. time for specialized fixed receivers.

Find the clock error that makes the circles meet

A flat, two-dimensional version of the problem. In two dimensions there are three unknowns (x, y and the clock error), so three satellites are enough; in three dimensions the same logic needs four.

Every circle is 12.0 km too large (40 µs × 0.2998 km/µs), so the crossing points spread over 8.3 km instead of meeting at one point.

The geometry is simplified. Satellites are placed about 110 km away instead of 20,200 km so the circles fit, and the ranges have no noise. The one real relationship is the conversion 1 µs of clock error = 0.2998 km of range error. Without JavaScript the picture shows the default state: a 40 µs error with no correction.

Notice that a single number fixes all three circles at once. That is what makes the clock error solvable: it adds the same length to every range, while a position error changes each range differently depending on direction. Only one combination of position and clock error fits every satellite.

Why the clocks must be so precise

The phone can solve for its own clock error, but it cannot solve for errors in the satellites' clocks, because each satellite's time stamp is the reference the whole calculation is built on. If satellite 2's clock is 10 ns off, the phone simply computes a range that is 3 m wrong for that satellite alone, and nothing in the equations reveals it. So the satellites need clocks that stay accurate on their own, between updates from the ground, to within a few nanoseconds.

That is why they carry atomic clocks, which count the vibrations of cesium or rubidium atoms. Ashby's figure shows the difference in stability between these and the quartz oscillator in a typical receiver.

The phone gets away with quartz because it re-solves its clock error with every fix, many times a second. The satellites have no such luxury, so the burden of precision sits in space. Ground stations still monitor each satellite and upload the small corrections that end up in the navigation message's clock data.

Relativity is large enough to matter

Atomic clocks that hold a few nanoseconds a day expose an effect that ordinary clocks never would. Two parts of Einstein's theories pull in opposite directions on a GPS satellite. Special relativity says a moving clock runs slow. General relativity says a clock higher in a gravitational field runs fast, and the satellites sit far above the Earth's surface, where gravity is weaker. At GPS altitude the gravity effect wins.

The specification gives the net figure precisely. Satellite clock rates "are offset by Δf/f = -4.4647E-10" (IS-GPS-200N, §3.3.1.1). Over a day of 86,400 seconds, 4.4647 × 10−10 adds up to about 38.6 microseconds, and 38.6 µs of light travel is about 11.6 km. Ashby's magazine article makes the same point: without the correction, errors building up "in just one day would cause navigational errors of more than 11 km."

The fix is built into the hardware. Before launch, each satellite's clock is set to run slightly slow, at 10.2299999954326 MHz instead of 10.23 MHz, so that once in orbit it appears to tick at exactly 10.23 MHz as seen from the ground. This was tested rather than assumed. When the first cesium clock went to orbit on the NTS-2 satellite in 1977, engineers left it uncorrected for about 20 days and measured it running fast by 442.5 parts in 1012, against a predicted 446.5, roughly 1% agreement. A smaller correction remains because GPS orbits are slightly elliptical, so the speed and height vary around each orbit; the receiver applies that one using a formula in the specification.

What still limits accuracy

With the clocks handled, the remaining errors come from the path the signal travels and from where the phone sits. The white paper's error budget shows their typical sizes before any corrections.

The ionosphere, a layer of electrically charged gas high in the atmosphere, slows radio signals by an amount that depends on frequency, and the lower atmosphere adds its own delay. Receivers subtract a model of both, and receivers that hear two frequencies can measure the ionospheric part directly. Near the ground, buildings block signals or reflect them, so the phone may time a bounced signal that has traveled farther than the straight path. Engineers call this multipath.

The government's commitment covers only the signal itself: a global average user range error of at most 2.0 m, 95% of the time, and in practice about 0.64 m on a day it reported in 2021. What you get also depends on geometry, since satellites spread across the sky pin a position down better than satellites bunched together. GPS.gov says GPS-enabled smartphones are typically accurate to within a 4.9 m radius under open sky, and worse near buildings, bridges, and trees.

How your phone gets a fix in seconds

The navigation message is slow. At 50 bits per second, one 1,500-bit frame takes 30 seconds, and the satellite's orbit and clock data occupy the first three of its five subframes. A receiver starting from nothing has to receive those pieces from each satellite before it can compute anything, and a phone indoors or moving may lose bits along the way.

Phones avoid most of this wait with assisted GNSS. Instead of decoding the orbit data from the sky, the phone downloads it over the cellular or Wi-Fi network, along with the current time, a rough position from nearby cell towers, and an ionosphere model. The white paper states that "The main aim of A-GNSS is to reduce TTFF", the time to first fix. The phone still measures every delay from the satellites itself; the network only supplies data the satellites would otherwise have sent slowly.

Modern phones also listen to other satellite systems, Europe's Galileo, Russia's GLONASS, and China's BeiDou, which work on the same principle. More satellites mean better geometry and more chances that four are visible between buildings, but each extra system adds one more unknown, the time offset between its clock and GPS's, which the receiver also solves for or receives in the data.

Limits of this explanation

The positioning principle here is the same for every GPS receiver, but the numbers vary. The satellite count is a 2023 figure, the error budget is a generic one rather than a measurement of any phone, and the 4.9 m accuracy comes from open-sky tests that city streets and indoor use will not match. Phones also blend GPS with Wi-Fi, cell towers, and motion sensors, so the blue dot on your map is not always a pure satellite fix.
