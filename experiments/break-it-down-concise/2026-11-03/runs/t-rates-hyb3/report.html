The short answer

A central bank raises one short-term interest rate, and other borrowing rates follow. Borrowing costs more and saving pays more, so households and businesses spend less. With less spending, firms feel less pressure to raise wages and have less room to raise prices, so inflation slows. Each step waits on the one before: loan payments reset slowly, firms cut orders and hiring only after unsold stock piles up, and most change prices only occasionally. The largest effect on output comes about a year after a rise, and on inflation about a year after that.

What the central bank actually controls

A central bank sets one price, the policy rate on very short loans between banks; in the US it is the federal funds rate, charged on overnight loans. The Fed steers it mainly through the interest on reserve balances, the rate it pays banks on cash held at the Fed. No bank lends for less than it can earn risk-free there, so that rate sets a floor under short-term market rates.

Banks usually change their standard lending rates the same day, the Bank of England reports, so variable-rate loans respond at once. Fixed-rate loans change only at renewal, and long-term rates move with what markets expect short rates to average.

How a higher rate reduces spending

Suppose the policy rate rises one percentage point and car loans rise with it, from 7% to 8%. A five-year $30,000 loan then costs about $608 a month instead of $594. Few families change plans over $14, but across millions of borrowers some buy a cheaper car or keep the old one.

The Bank of England lists other routes. Savers are paid more to wait. Bond, share and house prices fall, so owners feel poorer and can borrow less against them. Loan-financed projects must earn more to be worth doing, so firms invest and hire less. The currency tends to rise, pulling spending toward imports. Savers living on interest gain, but across the economy spending falls. Economists call this whole chain the transmission mechanism.

How less spending lowers inflation

An economy can produce only so much at a normal pace, its potential output; the distance from actual output is the output gap. When buyers want more than that, firms compete for scarce workers with higher wages and pass the costs on in prices. When spending falls back, the pressure eases and prices rise more slowly.

In the car example, unsold cars pile up, so the dealer orders fewer and holds off on raising prices. The factory cuts overtime and posts fewer openings, workers win smaller raises, and with labor costs rising more slowly, firms have less reason to raise prices.

Expectations matter too, because wages and prices are set with an eye on expected inflation. What counts for spending is the real interest rate, roughly the stated rate minus expected inflation: a 10% loan costs about 0% in real terms if people expect 10% inflation, and 7% if they expect 3%. A central bank believed to keep inflation low lowers the expectations built into wages and prices.

Why it takes so long

Each link moves only after the one before, and three are slow on their own. Loan contracts reset late; the Bank of England notes "it may be several months before higher official rates affect the payments made by some mortgage-holders," and fixed-rate borrowers feel nothing until they renew. Inventories add a relay: slower sales leave retailers with excess stock, so they order less from distributors, who order less from producers, who cut output and jobs only once their own stocks pile up. And prices and wages are sticky; Governor Waller notes firms typically change prices about once a year, and he counts fixed-term wage contracts among the causes of delay.

So the effect builds and fades rather than switching on. In the Bank of England's simulation of a one-point rise held for a year, output falls most, by 0.2% to 0.35% of GDP, after about five quarters. Inflation is "little changed during the first year," falls most, by 0.2 to 0.4 percentage points, after about nine, and is still lower three years on (charts on page 170 of the report↗). In US data, Arnaut and Bengali find the most rate-sensitive prices drop below their no-rise path about 18 months after a rise, and overall prices after a little over 24.

How big the effect is, and where it stops

One modest rise does little alone. Arnaut and Bengali estimate US prices are about 2.5% lower four years after a one-point rise than otherwise, with wide uncertainty; real tightening cycles are usually larger or longer.

Rates work on demand, not supply. Supply shocks such as crop failures or an oil disruption, the Fed's guide notes, raise prices and cut output at once, and a higher rate cannot grow wheat; it can only cut spending elsewhere. Food and energy prices also follow world markets more than US rates.

The delay itself varies, which is why economists speak of long and variable lags. Governor Waller argues that announcing rises in advance moves loan rates early; the two-year Treasury yield rose from about 0.25% to about 2% between September 2021 and March 2022, before the first hike, which he says cut roughly six months off the usual lag. Doh and Foerster estimate inflation's peak response came after more than three years before 2009 and about one year since, though they call the recent estimate highly uncertain.

This is why central banks act on forecasts. A rise today mostly affects inflation one to two years out, so waiting for high inflation means acting late, and raising until inflation falls risks overdoing it while earlier rises are still arriving.
