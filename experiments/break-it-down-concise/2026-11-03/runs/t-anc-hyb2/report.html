The short answer

Sound is a fast wobble in air pressure, and where two sounds meet their pressures add. If the speaker pushes the air out exactly when the noise pulls it in, the two cancel. "Exactly" means within a small fraction of one cycle. A low engine hum has long, repeating cycles, so there is timing slack and the next cycle is predictable. A voice has short cycles that change every syllable, so the same tiny delay spoils cancellation. Above about 1 kHz (1,000 Hz), headphones rely on the ear cup simply blocking sound.

Sound is pressure, and pressures add

A sound wave is air pressure rising and dipping many times a second; the count per second is its frequency, in hertz (Hz). Sound travels about 343 metres per second, so one cycle of 100 Hz spans 3.4 m of air (its wavelength), while one cycle at 4 kHz spans under 9 cm.

When two sounds reach the same point, the pressures there add, which is called superposition. A wave shifted by half a cycle, peaks on troughs, is in opposite phase, and adding it to the original gives zero: destructive interference. Paul Lueg patented using this against noise in 1934.

Inside the headphone: two microphones and a speaker

A noise-cancelling headphone plays anti-noise through its ordinary speaker. An outer reference microphone hears the noise before it leaks in; an inner error microphone, next to your ear, hears what is left. Feedforward control computes anti-noise from the outer microphone; feedback control keeps pushing the inner microphone's leftover toward zero; hybrids do both.

Timing is everything, and short waves leave no slack

The anti-noise must reach the ear at the same instant as the noise, so the microphone, converters, processor, and speaker must all finish first. Shi and colleagues call this "a causality constraint," and note it is tightest in headphones, where the microphones sit centimetres apart. Rivera Benois and colleagues found ordinary audio converters slow enough to "substantially decrease the noise attenuation frequency bandwidth."

What counts is the delay as a share of one cycle. Suppose the anti-noise is 20 microseconds (µs) late. That is 0.2% of a 100 Hz cycle, and the hum drops about 38 decibels (dB; every 10 dB is a tenfold cut in sound power). It is 8% of a 4 kHz cycle, and the "s" only drops to half its pressure, 6 dB. A delay of half a cycle lands peak on peak and doubles the noise.

The quiet spot shrinks as the pitch rises

Cancellation is complete only where the error microphone measures it. Nearby, the two sounds have travelled different distances and drift out of step. The KU Leuven notes give the rule of thumb of a 10 dB zone "approx λ/10" across, a tenth of a wavelength. That is 34 cm at 100 Hz, which easily covers microphone and eardrum, but under 1 cm at 4 kHz, so the microphone can be quiet while your eardrum is not. The rule comes from large rooms, so inside a cup the numbers differ, but not the trend.

Why a hum is easy and a voice is hard

Engines and propellers put most of their noise into one rotation-linked tone and its multiples, "the fundamental and harmonics of the blade passing frequency," in Shi and colleagues' aircraft example. A tone repeats every cycle, so a controller can produce anti-noise for a cycle it has not heard yet, and its delay stops mattering. With delay in the loop, the KU Leuven notes say, the controller "must act as a predictor." Random, changing sound cannot be predicted, so only its long, low cycles survive the delay.

A voice fails on both counts. Its base pitch, near 100 Hz for a man, is within reach, but it stacks components up to several kilohertz, the hiss of "s" peaks around 3–8 kHz, and the pattern changes every syllable.

What real headphones add up to

The active part covers the low end, and the cup or ear tip blocks the rest: passive attenuation "is effective in the mid and high frequency ranges," Rivera Benois and colleagues write, while the low range "is actively treated." The KU Leuven notes give 10–15 dB from 30 to 500 Hz as typical for feedback headsets.

These numbers come from prototypes and aviation headsets, not current earbuds, and Shi and colleagues note most commercial headphones use fixed rather than learning filters. With one fixed noise source, a feedforward prototype did cut random noise unevenly up to about 7.5 kHz. But with moving heads, shifting fit, and noise from all sides, the reliable active range stays low, and the seal decides how much of a nearby conversation you hear.
