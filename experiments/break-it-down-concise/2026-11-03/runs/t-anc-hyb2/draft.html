How headphones cancel noise by adding sound.

The short answer

Sound is a rapid wobble in air pressure, and when two wobbles meet, the pressures add. If the headphone's speaker pushes the air outward at exactly the moments the noise pulls it inward, the two cancel and the eardrum barely moves. The catch is the word exactly. The anti-noise has to match the noise in timing to a small fraction of one cycle. A low engine hum has long, slow cycles and repeats itself, so the headphone has plenty of slack and can predict what comes next. A voice is full of short, fast cycles that change every syllable, so the same small timing error that is harmless against the hum ruins cancellation of speech. Above roughly 1 kHz, headphones fall back on the ear cup and tips simply blocking sound, which is why voices still get through.

Sound is pressure, and pressures add

A sound wave is a pattern of air pressure rising slightly above and dipping slightly below normal atmospheric pressure, many times a second. Your eardrum follows that pressure. The number of rises per second is the frequency, measured in hertz (Hz): an engine hum might be 100 Hz, while the hiss in an "s" sits at several thousand hertz. The distance one cycle covers in air is the wavelength. Sound travels about 343 metres per second, so a 100 Hz cycle is about 3.4 m long and a 4,000 Hz (4 kHz) cycle is under 9 cm.

When two sounds reach the same point, the air there feels both pushes at once, and the pressure is simply their sum. This is called superposition. If one wave is at a peak exactly when another is at an equally deep trough, they sum to zero. That is destructive interference, and it is the whole trick. "Opposite phase" (or 180° out of phase) means the second wave is shifted by exactly half a cycle, peaks on troughs.

Paul Lueg patented this idea in 1934, long before electronics could do it well. His patent describes picking up the noise with a microphone and replaying it through a loudspeaker "in the form of sounds having an opposite phase."

Inside the headphone: two microphones and a speaker

A noise-cancelling headphone does Lueg's job in a few centimetres. It has up to two microphones on each side and uses its ordinary music speaker to play the anti-noise. The outer microphone, called the reference microphone, sits on the outside of the ear cup and hears noise before it leaks in. The inner microphone, called the error microphone, sits between the speaker and your ear and hears whatever is left after cancellation.

The two microphones give two strategies. Feedforward control uses the outer microphone: it hears the noise early, calculates what that noise will look like once it has leaked through the cup, and plays the inverse. Feedback control uses the inner microphone: it measures the leftover noise at the ear and keeps pushing to drive it toward zero, like a thermostat for pressure. Hybrid headphones run both. In feedforward designs, the inner microphone's leftover signal is also used to keep tuning the calculation, so the system can adjust as the fit or the noise changes. Shi and colleagues note that most commercial headphones skip this and use fixed filters, because cost, size, and battery power limit the processor.

Timing is everything, and short waves leave no slack

The anti-noise is only useful if it arrives at the ear at the same instant as the noise it targets. That means the microphone, the converter that turns sound into numbers, the processor, the converter back to sound, and the speaker must all finish before the noise itself gets there. Shi and colleagues put it plainly:

"This requirement poses a causality constraint on practical ANC systems."

Shi, Lam, Gan, Cheer, and Elliott, 2023

They add that headphones make this harder, because the outer microphone is only a few centimetres from the inner one, which leaves very little time to compute. Rivera Benois and colleagues report that standard audio converters and general-purpose processors add enough delay to "substantially decrease the noise attenuation frequency bandwidth," and they had to build a custom circuit with a total delay of 2.03 microseconds to match old-fashioned analogue circuits.

Why does a delay hurt high frequencies more? Because what matters is the timing error as a fraction of one cycle. Suppose the anti-noise is late by 20 microseconds (µs), a millionth of a second each. A 100 Hz hum has a cycle 10,000 µs long, so 20 µs is 0.2% of a cycle: the peaks and troughs still line up almost perfectly. A 4 kHz consonant has a cycle only 250 µs long, so the same 20 µs is 8% of a cycle, and the anti-noise trough now lands noticeably beside the noise peak. The two no longer cancel; they leave a residue about half as loud as the original. Push the error to half a cycle and the anti-noise lands peak-on-peak, doubling the noise instead of removing it.

Two things stand out when you move the sliders. First, the damage depends only on frequency multiplied by delay, so a delay ten times smaller buys cancellation up to a frequency ten times higher. That is why engineers chase microseconds. Second, the reduction falls off steeply: a 10 dB reduction (noise at roughly a third of its original pressure) needs the timing to be right within about 5% of a cycle, and a 20 dB reduction needs about 1.6%.

The quiet spot shrinks as the pitch rises

Even with perfect timing, cancellation is perfect only where the error microphone measures it. A short distance away, the noise and the anti-noise have travelled slightly different paths, so their timing no longer matches. The KU Leuven course notes give the usual rule of thumb: a zone with at least 10 dB of reduction about one tenth of a wavelength across ("10dB reduction in zone approx λ/10").

At 100 Hz, a tenth of a wavelength is about 34 cm, far bigger than an ear canal, so the microphone and your eardrum sit comfortably inside the same quiet spot. At 4 kHz it is under 1 cm, so a microphone sitting even a short way from the eardrum can be in a quiet spot while your eardrum is not. This rule comes from measurements in large reverberant rooms; inside a small sealed cup the numbers differ, but the direction holds: higher frequencies give a smaller zone.

Why a hum is easy and a voice is hard

An engine, a propeller, or a fan makes most of its noise at one rotation-linked frequency and its multiples. Shi and colleagues describe aircraft cabin systems that target "the fundamental and harmonics of the blade passing frequency." A tone like that repeats the same shape every cycle. That changes the timing problem completely: if the next cycle will look like the last one, the controller does not need to react to the noise. It can generate the anti-noise for a cycle it has not heard yet, and its own delay stops mattering. The KU Leuven notes make the same point from the other side: with delay in the loop, the controller "must act as a predictor" of the noise.

Random, constantly changing sound cannot be predicted that way, so for it the delay is a real error and only the long, low-frequency cycles survive it. The clearest test of this is a measurement where both kinds of noise are played at the same headphone at once.

The bump near 2.6 kHz in panel (a) matters too. Pushing noise down in one band came with pushing it up just outside, and the group's 2018 prototype showed similar amplification at the edges of its working range. That is one more reason designers keep the active band where the timing works.

A voice is the opposite of an engine on both counts. It does start low: a man's voice has its basic pitch near 100 Hz, inside the range ANC handles. But it stacks many higher components on top, up to several kilohertz, and the hiss of consonants like "s" peaks between about 3 and 8 kHz. And it never settles: the pattern changes with every syllable, with silences in between.

So headphones partly cancel the low part of a voice, but the parts that make speech understandable sit where the timing slack and the quiet zone have both run out, and they change too quickly to predict. Shi and colleagues record the same pattern in cars: engine-noise cancellation reached luxury cars years before the first mass-produced system for broadband road noise arrived in 2018.

What real headphones add up to

Because the active part covers only the low end, every noise-cancelling headphone relies on plain blocking for the rest. The ear cup or tip acts as a barrier, and barriers stop short waves well and long waves poorly. Rivera Benois and colleagues summarise the split: the materials' passive attenuation "is effective in the mid and high frequency ranges," and the low range "is actively treated." The KU Leuven notes give a typical figure for feedback headsets of 10–15 dB from 30 to 500 Hz, and the 2017 prototype above reached 10–20 dB up to about 870 Hz.

Two limits change how to read these numbers. They come from lab prototypes and older aviation headsets, not from current consumer earbuds, whose published measurements were not accessible for this report. And the boundary is not fixed: in a controlled test with one noise source in one position, a feedforward prototype reduced random noise unevenly up to about 7.5 kHz, so better electronics and a predictable setting can push the edge higher. In everyday use, with moving heads, shifting fit, and noise from every direction, the reliable active range stays low, and the passive seal decides how much of a nearby conversation you hear.
