Why a higher score does not prove the app helped.

Sources

  • Causal Inference: What If↗ Miguel A. Hernán and James M. Robins · Textbook, Chapman & Hall/CRC, free online edition · 2020, revised August 2026

    The standard graduate textbook by two Harvard epidemiologists who helped build modern causal methods; it supplies the core definitions and the case for randomization. Its examples are invented and medical, so they illustrate logic rather than measure anything about apps.

  • Causal Inference: The Mixtape↗ Scott Cunningham · Textbook, Yale University Press, free online first edition · 2021

    A widely taught economics textbook whose ten invented patients, with outcomes known both ways, show the exact arithmetic of selection bias. It teaches the mechanism, not the size of the bias for any real app.

  • Effectiveness of Reading and Mathematics Software Products: Findings from the First Student Cohort↗ Mark Dynarski, Roberto Agodini, and colleagues · Evaluation report to Congress, U.S. Department of Education, Institute of Education Sciences · March 2007

    A congressionally mandated randomized trial of 16 commercial reading and math products in 132 schools, run by two independent research firms. It covers those products in their first year of modest use, not learning software in general.

  • Disrupting Education? Experimental Evidence on Technology-Aided Instruction in India↗ Karthik Muralidharan, Abhijeet Singh, and Alejandro J. Ganimian · Peer-reviewed journal article, American Economic Review · Working paper December 2016, published April 2019

    A peer-reviewed lottery experiment by economists who run large school trials in India, testing an after-school program built around adaptive software. It covers 619 Delhi students in a program that also added a tutor and extra time.

  • Evaluating the Econometric Evaluations of Training Programs with Experimental Data↗ Robert J. LaLonde · Peer-reviewed journal article, American Economic Review · September 1986

    A landmark test of whether statistical adjustment can recover a known experimental answer, using a randomized job-training program. It concerns job training with 1980s methods, and later methods did better on the same data.

  • Impact Evaluation in Practice, Second Edition↗ Paul J. Gertler, Sebastian Martinez, and colleagues · Practitioner handbook, World Bank and Inter-American Development Bank · 2016

    An openly licensed training handbook by program evaluators that summarizes each evaluation design and its assumptions. Its worked case uses invented data, so its numbers show how methods behave, not what any real program did.

  • Education Technology: An Evidence-Based Review↗ Maya Escueta, Vincent Quan, Andre Joshua Nickow, and Philip Oreopoulos · Working paper, National Bureau of Economic Research · August 2017

    A review by education economists of only randomized and similarly rigorous ed-tech studies. It is the working-paper version of a later journal review and counts studies rather than averaging their effects.

The short answer

App users and non-users are different people, so a higher average among users shows that the groups differ, not why. Students who choose the app may be more motivated or better prepared, and might have scored higher without it. To know whether the app helped, you would need each user's score in a world where they skipped it, which nobody can observe. The workaround is to let chance decide who gets the app: a lottery makes the groups alike on average in every trait, including ones nobody measured, so the difference in their averages estimates what the app did. Real learning software tested this way has produced both large gains and none.

The gap compares different students

Suppose the 200 students who use an app average 78 on the exam and the 300 who do not average 70. That 8-point gap is an association: app use and higher scores appear together in the data. It compares two different sets of students, and nothing guarantees they were alike before anyone opened the app. Hernán and Robins show how far this can mislead with 20 invented patients: 54% of the treated die against 43% of the untreated, yet their full table shows 10 of 20 would die either way, so the treatment does nothing. The treated were simply sicker to begin with.

What "the app helped" means

Take one student, Maya. She has a score she would get with the app and a score she would get without it, everything else the same. The app's effect on her is the difference. Only one of those scores ever happens; the other, called the counterfactual, belongs to a world that did not occur. Because every student's second score is missing, no individual effect can be read off the data.

Researchers therefore aim at the average causal effect: the average score if every student used the app minus the average if none did. That compares the same students in two worlds. The observed gap compares two slices of students in the real world.

Self-selection adds bias to the gap

When students choose for themselves, the gap splits into two parts:

observed gap = true average effect for users + selection bias

Selection bias is what the users would have scored without the app minus what the non-users scored. In this constructed example, the app adds exactly 2 points for everyone and the three most motivated students choose it.

Users average 80 and non-users 62, an 18-point gap. Two points come from the app; the other 16 are selection bias, since the users would have averaged 78 anyway. Selection can also hide or reverse a real benefit: in Cunningham's invented example, surgery adds 0.6 years of life on average, but the patients who chose it would have fared worse anyway, so the observed comparison says it costs 0.4 years.

Other routes lead to the same trap. Cause can run backward, when students already doing well enjoy the app and keep using it. A before-and-after comparison credits the app with growth that would have happened anyway. And "heavier users scored higher" is still a comparison of self-chosen groups; the federal evaluators below warned that such relationships in their own trial "do not have a causal interpretation."

"Controlling for" differences is not enough

Comparing users with non-users who share measured traits, such as prior grades or family income, removes only the measured differences. Motivation that nobody recorded remains, and without randomization the data cannot show whether any such hidden difference is left. LaLonde tested how much this matters. A job-training program had been run as a randomized experiment, so its effect on earnings was known, about +$886. He swapped in comparison groups from national surveys and applied the adjustments economists then used.

Adjustment shrank the errors without fixing them. Later methods came closer on the same data, so the lesson is that adjusted comparisons can be badly wrong in ways the data do not reveal, not that they always fail.

Let chance decide who gets the app

If a coin flip decides who gets the app, motivated students are as likely to land in either group, and so are students with any trait nobody recorded. The groups would then have scored the same without the app on average, so selection bias is zero on average and the gap estimates the app's average effect. In Hernán and Robins's words, "In ideal randomized experiments, association is causation."

Everything in this model is invented. Motivation is a random number from 0 to 1; the no-app score is 55 + 30 × motivation, give or take up to 5 points of luck; the app adds the same amount for everyone. At 100%, a student's chance of choosing the app equals their motivation.

With students choosing, the gap sits around 12 points, and drawing more students only pins that wrong number down more precisely. Setting the true effect to zero leaves a 10-point "benefit." With a coin flip the gap lands near the true effect, but any one draw can be off by luck, more so with 20 students than 2,000. That is why real trials check that the groups started out alike and report a p-value: how often luck alone would produce a gap this large if the app did nothing. A p-value addresses luck, not bias, so a self-selected comparison can be highly "significant" and still wrong.

What randomized tests of learning software found

Education studies report gains as an effect size: the score difference divided by the typical spread of scores, the standard deviation (SD). One review's rule of thumb calls under 0.10 SD small and 0.25 to 0.40 large.

The U.S. federal study randomly assigned teachers to use one of 16 commercial products or teach as usual, and tested about 9,400 students. Its randomly formed classrooms started out largely alike, including on fall reading tests, and after one year they still scored alike.

The products filled roughly a tenth of instructional time in their first year, and a second-year follow-up found one of ten products with a significant positive effect. The Delhi lottery study found the opposite: 314 of 619 applicants won free seats in an after-school program built around adaptive software.

Across 29 randomized trials of learning software in wealthy countries, Escueta and colleagues counted 20 positive results, mostly in math, 8 with no effect, and 1 negative. The answer depends on the product and setting, so it has to be tested.

What real trials have to handle

Lottery winners in Delhi attended only 58% of the time, so comparing everyone offered with everyone not offered measures the effect of the offer; estimating the effect of actual use takes an extra step with its own assumptions, while comparing frequent with rare attenders brings self-selection back. Students drop out of follow-up unevenly (84% of winners and 88% of losers were tested at the end), control groups may use similar tools, and each trial covers particular products, places, and tests. Neither study tested a self-study smartphone app.

None of these assumptions can be fully verified. In the handbook's invented case, applied to the same data, the designs give estimates on both sides of the randomized answer, enough to flip its expand-or-not decision.

What to ask of the next claim

Ask who the users were compared with and how that comparison group was chosen. If students chose the app themselves, the gap mixes the app's effect with whatever made them different, and adjustment cannot prove the two have been separated. If a lottery decided, ask whether the groups started balanced, how large the effect is, whether it exceeds what luck would produce, and how many students actually used the app. The evidence here comes from classroom software, an after-school program, and job training; it shows that real software can produce large gains or none, not whether any particular app works.
