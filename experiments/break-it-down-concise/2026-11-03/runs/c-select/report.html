Did the learning app help? Causal inference from first principles.

Sources

Causal Inference: What If↗ Miguel A. Hernán and James M. Robins · Textbook, living online edition · Chapman & Hall/CRC · 2020, revised August 2026

A free graduate textbook by two Harvard epidemiologists whose work built much of the modern way of defining cause and effect. Its opening chapters supply the definitions used here and the reasons random assignment works and statistical adjustment rests on an assumption no one can check. Its examples are invented medical cases, so they show logic, not anything measured about apps.

Causal Inference: The Mixtape (Chapters 4 and 5)↗ Scott Cunningham · Textbook, free online first edition · Yale University Press · 2021

A Baylor economist's widely taught textbook. It works through ten invented patients whose outcomes are known both ways, which lets it show exactly how self-sorting can make a helpful treatment look harmful. The patients are made up, so the example teaches the mechanism and says nothing about how large the distortion is for a real app.

Effectiveness of Reading and Mathematics Software Products: Findings from the First Student Cohort↗ Mark Dynarski, Roberto Agodini, and colleagues (Mathematica Policy Research and SRI International) · Evaluation report to Congress · U.S. Department of Education, Institute of Education Sciences · March 2007

A national study ordered by Congress and run by two independent research firms: in 132 schools, teachers were randomly assigned to use one of 16 commercial reading or math programs or to teach as usual. It is the clearest real example here of a randomized test of learning software. It covers specific products in their first year, used about a tenth of class time, so it does not show that all software fails.

Disrupting Education? Experimental Evidence on Technology-Aided Instruction in India↗ Karthik Muralidharan, Abhijeet Singh, and Alejandro J. Ganimian · Peer-reviewed journal article · American Economic Review · April 2019

Economists who run large school experiments used a lottery to decide which of 619 Delhi applicants got free seats in an after-school program built around adaptive software. It shows a randomized comparison finding a large gain. The program bundled software with a tutor and extra hours, so the software's separate contribution is not isolated. Its figures were read from the 2017 working paper, whose numbers differ slightly from the published ones; the published figures are used here.

Evaluating the Econometric Evaluations of Training Programs with Experimental Data↗ Robert J. LaLonde · Peer-reviewed journal article · American Economic Review · September 1986

A labor economist took a job-training program whose true effect was known from a randomized experiment, swapped in comparison groups from national surveys, and applied the standard statistical adjustments. It is the best real test here of how adjusted comparisons perform against a known answer. It concerns job training, not schooling, and later methods did better on the same data.

Impact Evaluation in Practice, Second Edition↗ Paul J. Gertler, Sebastian Martinez, Patrick Premand, Laura B. Rawlings, and Christel M. J. Vermeersch · Practitioner handbook · World Bank and Inter-American Development Bank · 2016

An openly licensed training text by economists who evaluate government programs. It is used here for its plain account of tempting but misleading comparisons and its summary of the alternatives to randomizing. Its running case is fictional, so its numbers show how methods behave, not what any real program did.

Education Technology: An Evidence-Based Review↗ Maya Escueta, Vincent Quan, Andre Joshua Nickow, and Philip Oreopoulos · Review, working paper · National Bureau of Economic Research · August 2017

Education economists collected only randomized and similarly rigorous studies of education technology. It is used for the count of what such trials found and for a scale for judging effect sizes. It counts studies rather than averaging their results, and its later journal version, which may have updated counts, was not read.

The short answer

Higher scores among app users compare two different groups of students, not the same students with and without the app. Students who choose an app tend to differ from those who do not, in motivation, preparation, or time, and those differences raise scores on their own. The gap therefore mixes whatever the app did with how the groups already differed, and the data cannot tell you how much is which. To find out, let chance decide who gets the app, as with a lottery. Chance makes the two groups alike on average in everything, including traits nobody measured, so the gap that remains estimates the app's effect. Real trials done this way have found everything from no detectable gain to a large one, which is why the answer has to be measured.

What “the app helped” means

The app helped a particular student if that student scored higher with it than the same student would have scored without it, with nothing else changed. That difference is the app's causal effect on that student. The trouble is that each student either used the app or did not, so we see one of the two scores. The other, the score in the world that did not happen, is called the counterfactual, and it is missing for everyone. Hernán and Robins treat this missing score as the core difficulty of the whole subject.

Since no single student's effect can be seen, we ask about a group. The average causal effect compares two imagined worlds for one group of students: everyone uses the app, versus no one does. The comparison we actually have is different. It splits the real group into those who used the app and those who did not and compares those two subsets. Statisticians call that an association. It describes the world as it is, while the causal question asks a “what if.”

The two numbers can disagree sharply. In a made-up population of 20 patients, Hernán and Robins show 54% of the treated dying against 43% of the untreated, although the treatment changes nobody's outcome: in the full “what if” table, 50% die either way. The treated patients were simply sicker to begin with.

Where the gap comes from when students choose

When students pick for themselves whether to use the app, the gap between users and non-users breaks into two parts. One is the app's real effect. The other, called selection bias, is how differently the two groups would have scored if nobody had the app. Suppose six students take the exam, and the app raises every student's score by exactly 2 points. The three most motivated choose the app.

Users average 80 and non-users 62, a gap of 18 points. The app's true effect is 2. The other 16 points are selection bias: users would have averaged 78 without the app, against the non-users' 62. So the 18-point gap equals 2 points of effect plus 16 points of difference that existed before anyone opened the app. Nothing in the last column reveals that split, because the “without app” scores of users are never observed.

Selection bias can also run the other way, so the gap can hide a real benefit or point in the wrong direction. Cunningham's ten invented patients each get whichever treatment suits them best. Surgery adds 0.6 years of life on average across all ten, yet the patients who chose surgery live 0.4 years less than those who did not, because the people who chose it would have done far worse without it. He describes the whole enterprise of causal inference as finding a strategy for “negating the role that selection bias is playing in estimated causal effects.”

Other ways a gap appears without the app helping

Choice is the main route, but not the only one. The cause can run backward: students who are already doing well may enjoy the app and keep using it, while struggling students give up on it, so high scores produce app use rather than the reverse. A before-and-after comparison of the same users fails differently, because scores rise over a term for many reasons. In the Delhi lottery described below, students who lost the lottery also improved, by 0.34 standard deviations in math, so counting all of the winners' improvement as the program's effect would have roughly doubled it.

Patterns in how much students use an app mislead in the same way as the original question. In the federal study described below, fourth-grade reading effects were larger where teachers reported more use, and the evaluators warned that “these relationships do not have a causal interpretation.” Heavier users chose to be heavier users, even inside an experiment.

Why “controlling for” differences is not enough

The usual response is to compare users and non-users who look alike on recorded traits such as past grades, age, or family income, by matching them or by a statistical adjustment called regression. That removes differences in what was recorded and nothing else. If motivation was never measured, it still differs between the groups. Hernán and Robins add that no one can check from the data whether such a hidden difference remains:

“Worse yet, even if conditional exchangeability … held, the investigators cannot empirically verify that is actually the case.”

Hernán and Robins, Causal Inference: What If, §3.2

Here “conditional exchangeability” means that, once the recorded traits are matched, the groups would have scored the same without the treatment. LaLonde tested how well adjustment works when the right answer is known. A job-training program had been run as a randomized experiment, and it raised trainees' 1978 earnings by $886.

Random assignment: the fair comparison

Random assignment fixes the problem at its source. If a coin flip or lottery decides who gets the app, motivation cannot influence who uses it, so motivated and unmotivated students land in both groups in about equal shares. The same holds for every trait, measured or not. Before the app is used, the two groups would score about the same, so selection bias is zero on average, and the difference in average scores estimates the app's average effect. Hernán and Robins put it in one line: “In ideal randomized experiments, association is causation.” The federal evaluators made the same point about teachers' unmeasurable qualities, such as “a teacher's ability to motivate students,” which were “controlled” because teachers were assigned at random.

Chance still leaves small differences, so trials check that the groups started level and report how uncertain their result is. In the federal study, randomly assigned first-grade classrooms scored 50.1 and 50.9 on a fall reading test before using any software, and the two recorded traits that differed (teachers' master's degrees and students' age) were, the report says, not significant once it allowed for having tested many traits; it adjusted for them anyway. A p-value measures how often chance alone would produce a gap as large as the one seen; a large p-value means the gap could easily be luck. It says nothing about whether a gap is causal, only whether it is bigger than chance.

What real trials of learning software found

Education studies report gains in standard deviations, a measure of how spread out scores are. On a typical test, one standard deviation is roughly the distance from the 50th to the 84th percentile. Escueta and colleagues offer a scale: under 0.10 is small, 0.10 to 0.25 encouraging, 0.25 to 0.40 large, and above 0.40 very large.

The federal study, with about 9,400 tested students, found that classrooms assigned to the software did not score measurably higher after a year. In first grade the effects ranged from −0.01 to 0.06 standard deviations, which the report translates as about 0 to 2 percentile points, and none was statistically significant. The same held for fourth-grade reading, sixth-grade math, and algebra.

A second-year follow-up found one of ten products with a statistically significant positive effect. The Delhi lottery found much more: winners scored 0.37 standard deviations higher in math and 0.23 higher in Hindi after 4.5 months, large on the scale above. Across 29 randomized trials of learning software in wealthy countries, Escueta and colleagues count 20 with positive results, most of them in math, 8 with no effect, and 1 negative. They restricted themselves to such trials because the field is, in their words, “flooded with observational research.” None of these trials tested a phone app that students use on their own, so they cannot say whether such an app works; each answer belongs to its own product and setting.

What a trial measures, exactly

A lottery decides who is offered the app, not who uses it. In Delhi, winners attended 58% of sessions on average. Comparing everyone offered with everyone not offered keeps the comparison fair and measures the effect of the offer; the authors estimate the effect of actually attending with an extra step that uses the lottery itself, and comparing heavy attenders with light ones directly would bring self-selection back. Students also drop out of follow-up testing, 16% of winners against 12% of losers in Delhi, which the authors address with reweighting and bounding checks. A fair comparison also needs the no-app group untouched by the app, for instance by not borrowing a friend's. And every result belongs to one product, place, and test: the federal evaluators warn that their findings do not support conclusions “beyond the study's context.”

Sometimes randomizing is impossible or unfair, and researchers look for a situation that approximates it. Each design trades the coin flip for an assumption that cannot be fully checked.

In the World Bank handbook's fictional case, one program run through every method gives estimates from −$6.65 to −$14.46 against a randomized −$10.14, and the decision to expand the program flips depending on which method is used. LaLonde's real data showed that the spread can be far wider.

A users-versus-non-users gap is therefore not evidence against the app either: the true effect could be larger, zero, or negative. What settles it is a comparison in which the groups were made alike before the app arrived, so the first question to ask of any claim that an app works is how the comparison group was chosen. Even a clean trial answers only for the product, the students, the test, and the stretch of time it studied.
