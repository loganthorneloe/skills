The short answer

Sound is a wave of pressure, and two pressure waves at the same spot simply add. If a headphone speaker produces a wave that is the exact mirror image of the incoming noise, high pressure where the noise has low pressure and the reverse, the two sum to almost nothing at your ear. The catch is that "exact" is very strict: the anti-noise has to arrive within a few degrees of the right moment in the wave's cycle. Any fixed delay in the electronics is a small slice of a slow wave's cycle but a big slice of a fast wave's cycle, so the same headphone that removes 20–30 dB of a low engine hum barely touches sounds above about 1 kHz. A hum is also steady and repetitive, while a voice puts much of its energy, especially the consonants that make words understandable, between 1 and 4 kHz and changes from one syllable to the next. Above that range the headphones fall back on plain physical blocking by the ear cups.

Sound adds up, so it can also subtract

A sound is a rapid rise and fall in air pressure that travels outward from its source. Your eardrum moves in and out with those pressure changes. The number of rises per second is the frequency, measured in hertz (Hz); a low engine hum might be around 100 Hz, while the hiss of an "s" sits in the thousands of hertz (kHz). Because sound travels at about 343 metres per second, a 100 Hz wave is 3.4 m from one peak to the next, and a 2 kHz wave only 17 cm.

When two sounds reach the same point, the air pressure there is the sum of the two. This is called superposition. If two equal waves line up peak to peak, the pressure swings double. If one is shifted by half a cycle, so its peaks fall on the other's troughs, they cancel. Engineers describe that shift as a phase of 180°, measuring position within one cycle in degrees from 0° to 360°. Cancelling a sound by adding its mirror image is called destructive interference, and the added sound is the anti-noise.

The idea is old. Paul Lueg's 1936 patent already described a microphone, an electronic circuit and a loudspeaker arranged to do this in a pipe, and Shi and colleagues note that "the principles of ANC described by Leug still form the backbone of ANC technology today."

What is inside a noise-cancelling headphone

A headphone does the same thing in a much smaller space. Tiny microphones listen to the noise, a chip computes the inverted signal, and the same speaker that plays your music also plays the anti-noise. The microphones can sit in two places, and the names of the two designs come from control engineering.

A feedforward microphone sits on the outside of the ear cup. It hears noise before the noise reaches your ear, which gives the chip a brief head start to prepare the anti-noise. A feedback microphone sits inside the cup next to the ear. It hears whatever noise is left after cancellation, and the system keeps pushing that leftover toward zero. Most premium headphones combine both, which is called a hybrid design.

The ear cups and cushions also block sound physically, just as earmuffs do. This is called passive isolation, and it works best on high frequencies. Active noise cancellation (ANC) is the electronic part added on top.

The anti-noise must be almost perfect

Cancellation is forgiving in principle and unforgiving in practice. If the anti-noise is slightly too quiet or slightly late, part of the noise survives. Skovorodnikov's chart shows how much survives for a pure tone, depending on how far the anti-noise misses on loudness (horizontal axis) and on timing, expressed as phase (vertical axis, where 180° is a perfect mirror image).

He puts the tolerance this way: a clearly audible 10 dB reduction needs "a magnitude mismatch of up to 2dB and phase deviation from the ideal of 180° up to 20°." Each of those errors alone costs about that much, so the two together do slightly worse. A decibel (dB) is a logarithmic measure of loudness: 10 dB less is a tenth of the sound power, which people hear as roughly half as loud, and 30 dB less is a thousandth.

The phase part of the chart follows from adding two waves. For anti-noise of equal size that misses the mirror image by an angle φ, the leftover power is 2 − 2·cos φ times the original. At 2° that is −29 dB, at 10° it is −15 dB, at 20° it is −9 dB, at 60° it is 0 dB (no change), and past 60° the headphone makes the sound louder.

Why the same delay hurts high pitches more

Every stage of the system takes time. The microphone converts sound to numbers, the chip filters them, and the speaker has to move air. Skovorodnikov's example microphone alone adds 24 microseconds (µs, millionths of a second), and he states that "lower system latency improves the high-frequency limit" of feedforward cancellation. Designers subtract whatever delay they can predict, using the head start that the feedforward microphone provides. Shi and colleagues point out that headphones are so small that this head start is tiny, which ends up "limiting the available processing time window." Whatever timing error remains is the problem.

A timing error converts into a phase error that grows with frequency. One full cycle is 360°, so a delay τ shifts a wave of frequency f by 360° × f × τ. Suppose 50 µs of timing error remains, which is about how long sound takes to travel 1.7 cm. For a 100 Hz hum, whose cycle lasts 10,000 µs, 50 µs is 1.8° of phase, and the anti-noise still removes about 30 dB. For a 2 kHz consonant, whose cycle lasts only 500 µs, the same 50 µs is 36°, and only about 4 dB is removed. At 3.3 kHz the error reaches 60° and cancellation stops altogether.

Halving the delay doubles the frequency where cancellation stops, which is why chip makers compete on latency. It never removes the pattern: for any fixed delay, low frequencies cancel deeply and high frequencies do not.

The feedback loop has to back off at high frequencies

The feedback microphone adds a second limit. Its signal goes to the chip, out of the speaker, and back into the same microphone, forming a loop. Turning up the loop's strength cancels more noise, but a loop with too much strength and too much delay starts to reinforce its own output instead of correcting it, and it can howl. To stay stable, the controller must cut its strength at high frequencies, and the price is that it slightly amplifies noise in the band where it hands over. Engineers call this the waterbed effect, because pushing the noise down in one band raises it in another.

Skovorodnikov notes that passive attenuation is strong at high frequencies and "helps to mitigate the waterbed effect": the ear cup muffles the band where the electronics make things slightly worse.

What measurements of real headphones show

RTINGS measured three current flagship headphones on an artificial head, with ANC on and off, using a steady rushing noise that contains every frequency (pink noise). Each product has a dark line (ANC off, so the ear cups alone) and a light line (ANC on). Lower means quieter.

The measurements line up with the reasoning above. The electronics own the bass, where the ear cups are weakest because long waves pass through materials easily. The cups own the treble. RTINGS sums it up: "ANC doesn't improve upper-mid and treble range attenuation over passive isolation." Their split also matches the sounds people meet: they place airplane noise and truck engine rumble in the bass and people talking in the mids and treble.

Why an engine hum is the easy case

Low pitch is only half of why hum cancels well. The other half is that it repeats. An engine, a propeller or a fan turns at a steady rate, so its sound is mostly a few steady tones: the rotation rate and simple multiples of it. Shi and colleagues describe aircraft cabin noise at "the fundamental and harmonics of the blade passing frequency," and note that adaptive cancellation of such tonal noise in a duct reached about 30 dB.

A repeating wave is predictable. The next cycle looks like the last one, so the system does not have to react instantly to something new. It only has to keep its inverted copy lined up, and an adaptive system can fine-tune that copy over many cycles. The worked example shows the timing side: a 100 Hz hum leaves room for 50 µs of error and still loses about 30 dB.

Why voices get through

A voice fails both tests. First, much of it sits too high. The spectrogram below plots one man speaking for three seconds, with time running left to right, frequency bottom to top, and loudness as colour.

The parts of speech that carry meaning sit mostly above where ANC works. Monson and colleagues recount that early telephone research found about 95% of speech understood when everything above 4 kHz was removed, which is why phone lines carried 300 Hz to 3.4 kHz. Much of that band, especially the consonants, lies above the roughly 1 kHz ceiling in the measurements, where the delay problem, the feedback loop's waterbed and the ear's own resonance around 2–3 kHz, which Skovorodnikov names as a limit on how well the filters fit, all work against cancellation. Shi and colleagues note, for quiet zones in car cabins, that the region where cancellation holds shrinks as frequency rises. Inside an ear cup the same physics means that at 2 kHz, with a wavelength of only 17 cm, small differences between where the microphone sits and where your eardrum is start to matter.

Second, a voice never repeats long enough to predict. Its pitch moves, and its sounds change every syllable. The voice's lowest harmonics do fall in the cancelled band, but they shift too quickly to track. Adaptive systems need time to settle: RTINGS found that one popular model, given a steady 1 kHz tone, took "around three full seconds to reach their maximum attenuation." Shi and colleagues add that most commercial headphones use fixed filters anyway, because a chip small and frugal enough for a headphone cannot run the better adaptive methods fast enough. So the voice's low parts are reduced somewhat by fixed filters, and its high parts are left to the ear cups, which is why speech sounds muffled rather than gone.

What this means when you wear them

Noise-cancelling headphones are best thought of as two systems. Electronics remove steady low sounds such as engines, air conditioning and road rumble, often by 20–30 dB. The ear cups and their seal handle everything above about 1 kHz, including most of what makes speech intelligible, by blocking it. A poor seal therefore lets voices in no matter how good the electronics are, and passive earmuffs beat most headphones in the mid frequencies for the same reason.

The limits are physical, not a shortage of effort. Faster chips and microphones push the ceiling upward by cutting delay, but any delay leaves the same pattern, and a sound that changes from moment to moment can never be predicted the way a hum can. Two smaller caveats follow from the same mechanism: when tuning is aggressive, a feedback system can add a little noise around 1–3 kHz, and wind hitting the outside microphones creates pressure changes the system tries to cancel, which RTINGS found can make some headphones louder with ANC on than off.
