The short answer

Every GPS satellite keeps broadcasting two things: the exact time each piece of its signal left, and where the satellite was at that moment. Your phone notes when the signal arrives. Travel time multiplied by the speed of light gives the distance to that satellite, and distances to several satellites whose positions are known can only all be true at one point.

The catch is timing. Light covers about 30 cm in a billionth of a second, so a clock that is off by a millionth of a second puts every distance off by 300 m. Your phone's clock is far worse than that, so it does not trust it. Instead it treats its own clock error as a fourth unknown next to the three coordinates of its position, and listens to at least four satellites to solve for all four. That trick works only if the satellites' own clocks are right to within a few billionths of a second, which is why they carry atomic clocks, are corrected from the ground, and are even adjusted for Einstein's relativity, without which they would gain about 38 microseconds a day.

1. What a satellite tells you

GPS satellites orbit about 20,200 km above the ground and circle Earth twice a day. The U.S. government commits to keeping at least 24 working satellites in six tilted orbital planes, and in July 2023 it was flying 31. The planes are spaced so that from virtually anywhere on Earth at least four satellites are above the horizon at the same time, and the whole method depends on that.

Each satellite carries atomic clocks and sends a radio signal tied to them. As Ashby describes it, the signal can be thought of as a stream of time stamps: particular, recognizable points in the signal are emitted at known instants. A message riding on the signal tells the receiver where the satellite was when each time stamp left and how to correct the satellite's clock. So for every satellite it hears, a receiver knows the "when" and the "where" of a transmission. What it has to measure is when that transmission arrived.

2. Turning time into distance

Radio waves travel at the speed of light, c, which is defined as exactly 299,792,458 metres per second. If a signal left at time tsent and arrived at treceived, the distance it covered is

distance = c × (t_received − t_sent)

Suppose a satellite is straight overhead, 20,200 km up. The signal takes 20,200,000 ÷ 299,792,458 ≈ 0.0674 seconds, about 67 thousandths of a second, to reach you. That is easy to measure roughly and hard to measure well, because the multiplier is enormous. One nanosecond (a billionth of a second) of timing error becomes about 0.30 m of distance error, which is where Ashby's rule of thumb comes from: an error of one nanosecond gives a position error of about 30 cm.

3. Where the distances meet

One distance on its own does not say much. If you are 21,000 km from a satellite, you could be anywhere on a sphere of radius 21,000 km centred on it. A second distance gives a second sphere, and two spheres meet in a circle. A third sphere cuts that circle at two points, and one of them is usually far out in space, so with perfect clocks three satellites would be enough. In algebra, each satellite i at a known position (xi, yi, zi) gives one equation for your unknown position (x, y, z):

√[(x − xᵢ)² + (y − yᵢ)² + (z − zᵢ)²] = c × (t_received − t_sent,i)

4. Your phone's clock is the fourth unknown

The equation above uses treceived, but your phone only knows what its own clock says. Phone clocks are quartz oscillators, steady over a second or so but nowhere near the billionth-of-a-second agreement with the satellites that the distances need. Call the phone clock's error δt. Every arrival time it records is off by that same amount, so every distance it calculates is off by the same b = c × δt. Ashby calls these raw, offset distances pseudoranges, and writes that this clock bias "is the reason why measurements from four satellites are needed for navigation."

The key is the word same. One clock timed every arrival, so one number, b, is wrong in every equation. That makes it a single extra unknown rather than a separate error per satellite:

√[(x − xᵢ)² + (y − yᵢ)² + (z − zᵢ)²] = ρᵢ − b for i = 1, 2, 3, 4, …

Here ρi is the pseudorange the phone measured to satellite i. There are four unknowns, x, y, z and b, so four satellites give four equations and a solution. Only one value of b lets all the spheres pass through a single point; any other value leaves them disagreeing. Finding that value is how the phone learns its own clock error, and as a by-product the solution gives accurate time as well as position.

5. How the phone actually solves the equations

The equations contain square roots, so they cannot be rearranged into a direct formula the way a pair of straight-line equations can. Both Ashby and the Navipedia authors describe the same standard approach. The receiver starts with a guess, such as its last known position or simply the centre of Earth, and a guess for the clock error. Over a small move from that guess, each distance changes almost in proportion to the move, so the equations can be replaced by straight-line approximations. Those form an ordinary system of four linear equations in four small corrections, dx, dy, dz and the clock correction, which the phone solves exactly as you would solve simultaneous equations in algebra class. It applies the corrections to get a better guess and repeats. Each round lands closer, and after a few rounds the corrections become negligible.

A phone usually hears more than four satellites. Then there are more equations than unknowns, and because every measurement carries some error, they cannot all be satisfied at once. The receiver uses least squares: it picks the position and clock error that make the leftover mismatches as small as possible overall, measured by the sum of their squares. Extra satellites therefore average down noise rather than going to waste.

6. Why the satellite clocks must be so good

The phone can solve away its own clock error because that error is shared by every equation. A satellite's clock error is different: it shifts only that satellite's distance, so it cannot be absorbed into b and lands directly in the position. At 30 cm per nanosecond, getting distances right to a metre or so means each satellite clock must be right to within a few nanoseconds.

Even 4 ns is about 1.2 m, so atomic clocks alone are not enough. Ground stations track each satellite's clock, and the satellite broadcasts the measured corrections in its navigation message so that receivers can remove what remains. The satellite clocks also have to be corrected for something much larger than their drift.

Relativity makes orbiting clocks run fast

Two effects from Einstein's theories change how fast a satellite's clock ticks compared with an identical clock on the ground. The first is that clocks deeper in a gravitational field run slower. A satellite is farther from Earth's mass than you are, so its clock runs faster than a ground clock, by 5.288 parts in 1010. Ashby calculates that, if ignored, this alone would build up in one day into a navigation error of 13.7 km. The second is that moving clocks run slower. The satellite moves faster than a point on the rotating ground, so this effect makes it run slower, by 0.8228 parts in 1010, worth about 2.13 km of error a day.

Subtracting the two gives a net gain of about 4.465 parts in 1010. Over a day of 86,400 seconds that is about 38.6 microseconds, and multiplied by the speed of light it is about 11.6 km, which matches 13.7 km minus 2.13 km. Against a timing budget of a few nanoseconds, this is thousands of times too large to leave alone. The fix is made before launch: the satellite clocks are tuned slightly low, so that seen from the ground they tick at the intended 10.23 MHz.

This was tested rather than assumed. When the NTS-2 satellite carried the first cesium clock into orbit in 1977, some engineers doubted the correction was real, so it was launched with a switchable adjustment. Over about 20 days its clock ran fast by 442.5 parts in 1012 against a predicted 446.5, agreeing with relativity to about 1%, and the correction was switched on.

7. What sets the accuracy you see

The government's commitment covers only the signal: across healthy satellites, the daily average error in the distance each signal implies (the user range error) must be no more than 2.0 m, 95% of the time. Actual performance is usually much better; on 20 April 2021 the global average was 0.643 m or less, 95% of the time. GPS.gov is explicit that "URE is not user accuracy." Smartphones are typically accurate to about 4.9 m under open sky, the average from the students' phones in the van Diggelen and Enge class, and worse near buildings, bridges and trees.

Several things widen the gap between a sub-metre range error and a several-metre position error. The first is geometry. When the satellites a phone uses are bunched in one part of the sky, the spheres cross at shallow angles, and a small error in a distance slides the crossing point a long way. The Navipedia authors define a number for this, the dilution of precision, which depends only on where the satellites are relative to you and acts as a multiplier from distance error to position error. The second is the atmosphere: the signal slows slightly in the charged upper atmosphere (the ionosphere) and in water vapour lower down, which makes distances look too long. Receivers that listen on two GPS frequencies can measure and remove most of the ionosphere's delay because it differs between frequencies; most single-frequency devices can only model it. The third is the local surroundings. Buildings block satellites, which worsens geometry, and signals that bounce off walls before arriving (multipath) take a longer path and look farther away. The class abstract reports that phones' urban accuracy tracked building height.

What this means, and where it stops

GPS turns a positioning problem into a timing problem. Your phone never sends anything to the satellites; it only listens, times arrivals, and solves four or more equations whose fourth unknown is its own clock. That is why a phone with a cheap clock can locate itself at all, and why the expensive precision sits entirely in the satellites and the ground system: their clocks have to agree to a few nanoseconds, after corrections for drift and for a relativistic gain of about 38 microseconds a day.

The same logic sets the limits. Anything that changes a signal's travel time without the receiver knowing, such as a bounce off a building or extra delay in the atmosphere, looks exactly like extra distance. That is why accuracy is best under open sky and degrades in cities, and why high-end receivers that use two frequencies and correction services reach centimetres while a typical phone works at a few metres. This report covers only the core GPS calculation. Modern phones also combine it with other satellite systems and with network-supplied help to get a fix faster, which this explanation does not examine.
