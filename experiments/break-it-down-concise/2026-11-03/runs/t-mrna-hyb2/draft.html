How mRNA vaccines work.

The short answer

The shot delivers a set of instructions, written in mRNA, for making one harmless piece of the virus: its spike protein. A few of your cells read those instructions and make spike for a short time. Your immune system meets that spike, treats it as foreign, and builds antibodies and memory cells that recognize it, so if the real virus arrives later, it is recognized and attacked quickly. The mRNA cannot change your DNA because it works only in the part of the cell outside the nucleus, where DNA is not, your cells have no ordinary way to turn mRNA back into DNA, and the cell breaks the mRNA down within days to a couple of weeks.

What you need from high-school biology

Your DNA is a permanent library of instructions, kept inside each cell's nucleus. To make a protein, the cell copies one instruction, a gene, from DNA into a short-lived working copy called messenger RNA, or mRNA. That copy leaves the nucleus and travels into the surrounding cytoplasm, where machines called ribosomes read it three letters at a time and string together the matching protein. The cell then destroys the mRNA, which is how it controls how much of each protein it makes.

Two features of this flow matter for everything that follows. Information moves from DNA to mRNA to protein, and the cell's normal machinery does not run it backward. And the reading of mRNA happens in the cytoplasm, outside the nucleus. An mRNA vaccine uses this machinery exactly as your own mRNA does, with one change: the copy comes from a lab instead of from your DNA.

What is in the shot

A virus such as SARS-CoV-2, the cause of COVID-19, is covered in a protein called spike, which it uses to latch onto and enter your cells. Spike is also what your immune system most needs to recognize, because antibodies that stick to spike can stop the virus from getting in. The COVID-19 mRNA vaccines therefore carry mRNA that spells out the spike protein, in a slightly modified form that holds the shape spike has before it fuses with a cell. According to the FDA briefing document, the Pfizer-BioNTech vaccine is spike-encoding RNA "formulated in lipid nanoparticles," given as two 30-microgram doses three weeks apart.

Bare mRNA is fragile and would be chewed up by enzymes in the body before reaching a cell. The lipid nanoparticle solves this: it is a tiny ball of fat-like molecules, about 100 nanometres across according to Pardi and colleagues, that wraps the mRNA and fuses with a cell's membrane, releasing the mRNA into the cytoplasm. Muscle cells near the injection site take it up, and so do immune cells there and in nearby lymph nodes.

One more problem had to be solved first. Your immune system has sensors that detect stray RNA, because foreign RNA inside a cell is a common sign of viral infection. Early lab-made mRNA set off those sensors, caused inflammation, and made little protein. Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman found that your own cells' RNA often carries chemically altered versions of the letter U, and that building lab mRNA with such altered letters (pseudouridine and its relatives) nearly abolished the inflammatory reaction and increased protein production. The Nobel committee illustrates this finding in its Figure 2↗; both COVID-19 mRNA vaccines use modified letters, and that work won the 2023 Nobel Prize.

How making spike teaches the immune system

Suppose the shot goes into your upper arm. A muscle cell there takes in a nanoparticle, its ribosomes read the spike mRNA, and it starts making spike protein. According to the CDC, the immune system then recognizes this protein as foreign and activates other immune cells against it. Immune cells that took up the vaccine themselves, especially dendritic cells, chop spike into fragments and carry them to the nearest lymph nodes, the bean-shaped filters in your armpit that drain the arm.

Inside the lymph node, two kinds of white blood cell matter most. B cells carry receptors shaped to grab particular molecules; the rare B cells whose receptors happen to fit spike are selected and multiply. T cells come in two main types: helper T cells, which coordinate the response, and killer T cells, which destroy infected cells. Pardi and colleagues explain that because the vaccine makes spike inside cells, those cells present spike fragments in the way that alerts killer T cells, as a real infection would, and also activate helper T cells.

The key training happens in small structures inside the lymph node called germinal centres. There, B cells that bind spike multiply and mutate their receptors, and a specialized helper cell, the T follicular helper cell, picks out the variants that grip spike best. Pardi and colleagues identify these helper cells as a population vaccines must activate to produce strong, long-lived antibodies. The winners become plasma cells that pour out antibodies, and memory B cells that stay behind after the response winds down.

Turner and colleagues found spike-binding germinal-centre B cells in every one of the 14 people they sampled, and these stayed at high levels for at least 12 weeks after the booster. This is the direct human evidence that a short burst of spike production leaves behind a long-running training process.

Evidence that it protects

When the real virus later arrives, antibodies already circulating can bind its spike and block it from entering cells, and memory B and T cells recognize it and respond faster than they could on a first encounter. The randomized trial reviewed by the FDA tested whether this works in practice. Among 36,621 participants without prior infection, there were 8 confirmed COVID-19 cases in the vaccine group and 162 in the placebo group from a week after the second dose, which the FDA reported as 95.0% efficacy.

Notice the delay. Protection did not appear when the mRNA was most abundant, in the first few days, but after the lymph nodes had had time to train B and T cells. That is exactly what the mechanism predicts: the mRNA is a temporary trigger, and the protection comes from the immune memory it leaves.

Why it does not change your DNA

For vaccine mRNA to alter your DNA, three things would all have to happen, and the cell's normal biology blocks each one.

It would have to reach the DNA

DNA sits inside the nucleus, behind its own membrane. The vaccine mRNA is released into the cytoplasm and read there; the CDC states that these vaccines "do not enter the nucleus of the cell where our DNA ... is located."

It would have to become DNA

RNA and DNA are different molecules, and a cell can only add something to its genome in DNA form. Turning RNA into DNA requires an enzyme called reverse transcriptase, which HIV and similar retroviruses bring with them. Ordinary cells do not use one to copy their mRNA.

It would have to be cut in

Even DNA floating in a nucleus does not insert itself; retroviruses carry a second enzyme, integrase, to splice their DNA into a chromosome. The vaccine carries only the spike instructions and no enzymes.

And it would have to last

The cell breaks vaccine mRNA down with the same machinery it uses on its own mRNA, so the instructions disappear rather than accumulate.

Pardi and colleagues summed this up before the COVID-19 vaccines existed by calling mRNA a "non-infectious, non-integrating platform." That design is also why it cannot cause infection: the shot contains instructions for one protein, not a virus that can copy itself.

"Breaks down quickly" needs a number. Fertig and colleagues measured vaccine mRNA in the blood of 16 vaccinated people and found traces for about two weeks.

So "gone in hours" overstates it; "gone within days to a few weeks" is accurate. Lasting longer does not open a route to DNA, because the barriers above do not depend on time.

The one published challenge deserves a precise description. Aldén and colleagues soaked a line of liver cancer cells in the vaccine and reported finding a DNA copy of the vaccine sequence within six hours, which they attributed to LINE-1, a stretch of the human genome that can make its own reverse transcriptase. They also wrote, "we do not know if DNA reverse transcribed from BNT162b2 is integrated into the cell genome." Merchant, in a comment in the same journal, argued that cancer cells grown in a dish differ in exactly the ways that matter, that the exposure was far higher than a liver would receive, and that in a body the immune system clears cells making spike. No study read for this report showed vaccine sequences inserted into the genome of a vaccinated person.

What this means, and its limits

The mRNA vaccine is a way of letting your own cells manufacture a vaccine protein briefly, so the immune system can train on it, after which the instructions are destroyed. The protection comes from immune memory, which is also why it fades: antibody levels fall over months, and when the virus's spike changes enough, as it has with later variants, older antibodies fit less well and updated vaccines carry updated spike instructions. The trial data here are from 2020 and the original virus, and the lymph-node and blood studies are small; they show how the mechanism works, not how strong protection is today.

The DNA question rests on the basic one-way flow of genetic information plus the specific barriers above. The lab study that found a DNA copy in cancer cells did not show any change to a genome, and none has been shown in vaccinated people.
