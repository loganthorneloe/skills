The short answer

Each GPS satellite broadcasts the time on its atomic clock and where it is. Your phone notes when each broadcast arrives; travel time multiplied by the speed of light gives a distance, and distances to satellites at known positions pin down one point.

Light covers 30 cm in a nanosecond, so a clock off by a microsecond makes every distance 300 m wrong. Your phone has no clock that good. It treats its own clock error as a fourth unknown next to the three position coordinates, which is why it needs four satellites. The satellites cannot use that trick, so they carry atomic clocks kept in step to nanoseconds and corrected even for relativity.

What each satellite sends

About 31 working satellites orbit roughly 20,200 km up in six planes, arranged so at least four are above the horizon from virtually anywhere (GPS.gov↗, count as of July 2023). Each broadcasts a stream of timing marks, each with a message saying when it was sent and where the satellite was, from an orbit description called the ephemeris. Ground stations predict each orbit and clock a few hours ahead and upload the predictions for rebroadcast.

Time becomes distance

Radio travels at c = 299,792,458 m/s, so distance = c × (arrival time − send time). From a satellite directly overhead the signal takes about 67 milliseconds. A microsecond of timing error is 300 m of distance, and, as Ashby puts it, timing errors of one nanosecond lead to positioning errors of about 30 cm. A position good to a few metres needs timing good to around ten nanoseconds.

From distances to a position

One distance puts you on a sphere around a satellite, two narrow you to a circle, and a third cuts that circle at two points, one usually far off in space. With perfect clocks, three satellites would do. Ashby writes the problem as one equation per satellite j, at known position rj, sending at known time tj:

c2(t − tj)2 = |r − rj|2,   j = 1, 2, 3, 4

Both sides are the distance squared, once from light travel and once from geometry. The reception time t is unknown and appears in every equation beside x, y, z. Four unknowns need four equations; Ashby notes these four "can be solved for the unknown space-time coordinates" of the reception.

Your phone's clock is the fourth unknown

The reception time is unknown because the phone keeps time with a quartz oscillator, like a wristwatch, that is not in step with GPS time to the microsecond. So each distance it computes is the true distance plus one offset, c × b, where b is the phone's clock error. This measurement is called a pseudorange. Every signal is timed against the same phone clock, so every pseudorange is off by the same amount, and a shared error can be solved for. With b as the fourth unknown, the phone learns where it is and the exact time. In practice receivers refine a guess using every satellite in view.

Ignoring the clock, 1 µs of error moves the fix about 400 m, more than the 300 m range error, and almost straight down: with every satellite overhead, stretching all distances equally looks like being lower. In real GPS height is likewise usually the weakest coordinate. Solving for the clock removes the error exactly.

Why the satellites need atomic clocks

That trick works only because the phone's error is shared. Each satellite's clock error lands in one distance only, where the equations cannot tell it from your position, so satellites must stay within nanoseconds of common GPS time.

Over a second quartz is steadier, enough for a phone timing one measurement, but beyond about 100 seconds it climbs far above cesium, which holds near 1 part in 1013 for days. Ashby puts a cesium clock left alone for a day at about 4 ns off, roughly 1.2 m of distance; the quartz curve at a day implies a few microseconds, close to a kilometre. The curves are nominal, and real clocks vary around them.

Relativity adds 38 microseconds a day

An orbiting clock moving at nearly 4 km/s runs slow (special relativity), and sitting higher in Earth's gravity runs fast (general relativity). At GPS altitude gravity wins.

Low orbits like the Shuttle's lose time, the curve crosses zero about 9,545 km from Earth's centre, and GPS clocks gain. Ashby's Equation 35 gives the GPS net as 4.4647 × 10−10 of a second per second: about 38.6 µs a day, or 11.6 km of light travel, against a ten-nanosecond budget. Most of it is removed before launch by building the clocks to run at 10.22999999543 MHz, so they tick at exactly 10.23 MHz as seen from the ground. Orbits are slightly elliptical, and the receiver corrects the leftover periodic effect from the broadcast orbit, up to about 23 ns for an eccentricity of 0.01. Without these corrections, Ashby writes, "the system would not work."

What limits your phone

Most remaining error comes from the signal's path. GPS.gov lists blockage by buildings, bridges and trees, indoor or underground use, and reflections off buildings (multipath) as the common causes.

Clean signals give mostly sub-metre fixes. A phone does worse; GPS.gov gives about 4.9 m under open sky as typical. A reflection arrives late, so its distance reads long, and unlike the phone's clock offset that error differs for each satellite and cannot be solved away. Government accuracy commitments, GPS.gov notes, cover the signal broadcast from space, not the position your device reports.
