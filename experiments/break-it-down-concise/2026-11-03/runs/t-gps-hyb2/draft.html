The short answer

Each GPS satellite continuously broadcasts two things: the exact time on its onboard atomic clock, and where it is in its orbit. Your phone notes when each broadcast arrives. Arrival time minus send time is the travel time, and travel time multiplied by the speed of light is a distance. Distances to several satellites whose positions are known pin down a single point, the way three measured distances to known landmarks pin down where you are on a map.

The catch is the phone's own clock. Light covers 30 centimetres in a billionth of a second, so a clock that is off by a millionth of a second puts every distance off by 300 metres. Your phone does not carry a clock that good. Instead it treats its own clock error as one more unknown and solves for it along with latitude, longitude and height. Four unknowns need four satellites. The satellites, on the other hand, really do need atomic clocks, kept in step to within nanoseconds and even corrected for relativity, because every metre of their timing error lands directly in your position.

What each satellite sends

GPS has about 31 working satellites in medium Earth orbit, roughly 20,200 km up, arranged in six orbital planes and each circling Earth twice a day. The arrangement is chosen so that at least four are above the horizon from virtually anywhere on the planet, according to GPS.gov↗ (count as of July 2023).

Each satellite carries atomic clocks and broadcasts a radio signal tied to them. Ashby describes the signal as a sequence of timing marks, each accompanied by a message stating when and where it was sent. The "where" comes from the satellite's ephemeris, a compact description of its orbit. Ground monitoring stations track every satellite, a master control station in Colorado predicts each orbit and clock for the next few hours, and those predictions are uploaded to the satellites for rebroadcast. So the satellite is not measuring anything about you. It is a clock and a position announcement, repeated continuously.

Time becomes distance

Radio waves travel at the speed of light, c = 299,792,458 metres per second. If you know when a signal left and when it arrived, the distance it travelled is

distance = c × (arrival time − send time)

Suppose a satellite is directly overhead, 20,200 km away. Its signal takes 20,200,000 ÷ 299,792,458 ≈ 0.0674 seconds, about 67 milliseconds, to reach you. That is easy to measure roughly. The hard part is how precisely. Multiply the speed of light by small time errors and you see the problem:

  • 1 millisecond of error is about 300 km.
  • 1 microsecond (a millionth of a second) is about 300 m.
  • 1 nanosecond (a billionth of a second) is about 30 cm.

Ashby puts the same rule plainly: timing errors of one nanosecond lead to positioning errors of about 30 cm. To get a position good to a few metres, the timing behind each distance has to be good to around ten nanoseconds. Everything else about GPS follows from that requirement.

From distances to a position

One distance tells you that you are somewhere on a sphere centred on that satellite. A second distance narrows you to the circle where two spheres intersect. A third cuts that circle at two points, one of which is usually far out in space or deep inside Earth and can be discarded. With perfect clocks, three satellites would be enough.

In algebra, satellite j sits at a known position rj and sent its mark at a known time tj. You are at the unknown position r = (x, y, z), and you received the marks at time t. Ashby writes the whole system as one line:

c2(t − tj)2 = |r − rj|2,   j = 1, 2, 3, 4

The left side is the light-travel distance squared; the right side is the geometric distance squared, (x − xj)2 + (y − yj)2 + (z − zj)2. Notice that the reception time t appears in every equation as an unknown, next to x, y and z. That is four unknowns, which is why the equation runs over four satellites. In Ashby's words, "these four equations can be solved for the unknown space-time coordinates" of the reception event.

Your phone's clock is the fourth unknown

Why is the reception time unknown? Your phone's timekeeper is a quartz oscillator, the same kind of crystal found in a wristwatch. Nothing keeps it in step with GPS time to the microsecond, and each microsecond it is off adds 300 m to every distance. So the distance a receiver computes is not the true distance. It is the true distance plus one shared offset, c × b, where b is how far the phone's clock is off. Engineers call this measured quantity a pseudorange.

The key word is shared. Every satellite's signal is timed against the same phone clock, so every pseudorange is wrong by the same amount. That makes the error something you can solve for rather than something you must prevent. Treat b as a fourth unknown next to x, y, z, measure four pseudoranges, and solve. The phone ends up knowing both where it is and what time it is, to within nanoseconds, without ever owning a precise clock. The equations are nonlinear because of the squares, so receivers solve them by starting from a guess and repeatedly correcting it, and they use every satellite in view, not only four, to average down noise.

Two things show up. First, ignoring the clock does not just add a little fuzz: 1 µs of clock error moves the computed position by about 400 m, more than the 300 m range error itself. Second, the error goes almost straight down. All the satellites are above you, so a uniform stretch of every distance looks, to the algebra, mostly like you being further from all of them at once, which means lower. In real GPS, with satellites spread above but never below you, height is likewise usually the weakest coordinate. Add the clock as an unknown and the error vanishes, because the shared offset is now accounted for exactly.

Why the satellites need atomic clocks

The trick of solving for the clock only works for the phone's clock, because that one error is shared across all the measurements. Each satellite's clock error is different and lands in only its own distance, so nothing in the equations can separate it from where you actually are. The satellites must therefore be synchronised to a common GPS time to within a few nanoseconds, all the time, while ground control uploads small corrections for each clock.

Read the plot from left to right. Over a second or so, quartz is actually the steadier clock, which is why a phone can time a signal well over the instant it measures. But by 100 seconds the quartz curve has risen far above cesium, and it keeps climbing. Cesium stays near 1 part in 1013 for days. Ashby works out what that means: a cesium clock left alone for a day should still be correct to about 4 nanoseconds, roughly 1.2 m of distance. Reading the quartz curve at a day (about 10−10.5) gives a few microseconds of drift, close to a kilometre of distance. The curves are nominal, and real clocks vary around them.

Relativity adds 38 microseconds a day

Even a perfect clock in orbit does not tick at the same rate as one on the ground. Two effects pull in opposite directions. The satellite moves at nearly 4 km/s, and moving clocks run slow (special relativity). It also sits higher in Earth's gravity, and clocks further from a mass run fast (general relativity). For a GPS orbit the gravity effect wins.

Notice where the curve crosses zero: about 9,545 km from Earth's centre, roughly 3,200 km up. Below that radius the speed effect dominates; above it the weaker gravity dominates. Ashby's Equation 35 gives the GPS value as a net 4.4647 × 10−10, meaning the satellite clock gains that fraction of a second every second. Over a day of 86,400 seconds that is about 38.6 microseconds, which at the speed of light is about 11.6 km of distance. Against a timing budget of ten nanoseconds, that is enormous.

GPS handles most of it before launch. The clocks are built to run at 10.22999999543 MHz instead of the nominal 10.23 MHz, so that, seen from the ground, they tick at exactly 10.23 MHz. Orbits are not perfect circles, so a satellite speeds up and dips lower near its closest approach, and the remaining periodic effect is corrected by the receiver itself from the broadcast orbit, up to about 23 ns for an orbit with eccentricity 0.01, by Ashby's calculation. Ashby's summary is that without carefully accounting for these effects "the system would not work."

What limits your phone

With the clocks and relativity handled, the remaining error comes mostly from the path the signal takes. GPS.gov lists signal blockage by buildings, bridges and trees, use indoors or underground, and signals reflected off buildings (called multipath) as the common causes of degraded accuracy.

The histogram shows what the system delivers when signals arrive cleanly: most fixes under a metre, with a thin tail. A phone does worse. GPS.gov gives a typical figure of about 4.9 m radius under open sky, worsening near buildings, bridges and trees. A reflected signal is the clock problem in another form: the arrival is late, so the computed distance is long, and unlike the phone's clock offset that error is different for each satellite and cannot be solved away.

GPS.gov also draws a distinction worth knowing when you read accuracy claims. The government's commitments cover the signal in space, measured as user range error (how wrong each satellite's distance is), not the position your device reports. Position accuracy depends on that, plus the receiver, the surroundings, and how the visible satellites are spread across the sky.
