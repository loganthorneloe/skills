# Source audit records

Claims
- C1: A central bank directly sets only one very short-term interest rate; that rate passes through to other market and bank rates.
- C2: Higher rates reduce household and business spending (cash flow on debt, reward for saving, investment hurdle, lower asset values, stronger currency).
- C3: Weaker demand relative to the economy's capacity reduces pressure on wages and prices, lowering inflation; a stronger currency also lowers import prices.
- C4: Inflation expectations matter: they set the real interest rate and feed into wage and price setting; credibility of the target anchors them.
- C5: Timing and size: activity responds first (peak about a year), inflation later (peak about two years); empirical average lag to the strongest price response is about 29 months, with wide variation; about 0.9% maximum price-level fall per 1 pp hike.
- C6: The lags come from slow retail-rate pass-through, fixed-rate debt, slow spending and investment decisions, inventory/production chains, and slow wage and price adjustment.
- C7: Individual prices change infrequently: US regular (non-sale) consumer prices last roughly 8 to 11 months.
- C8: Limits: monetary policy works mainly via demand, not supply capacity; lags are variable and uncertain.

### The transmission mechanism of monetary policy
- url: https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1999/the-transmission-mechanism-of-monetary-policy.pdf; text read from: same
- byline: Bank of England staff under the guidance of the Monetary Policy Committee · central bank report · Bank of England Quarterly Bulletin · May 1999
- verdicts: C1 pass; C2 pass; C3 pass; C4 pass; C5 conditional (model simulation of a temporary one-year 1 pp rise; illustrative, not a forecast; UK 1999 economy); C6 pass; C8 pass
- use: p.161 summary and transmission diagram; pp.161-164 short/long rates, asset prices, exchange rate; pp.164-166 individuals and firms; p.167 "Time-lags" and output gap; p.168 inflation expectations and real rates, imported inflation; p.170 Charts 1-2 simulation.
- credibility: Written by the staff of the central bank that sets UK rates, describing its own policy committee's view at the request of parliamentary committees; firsthand knowledge of operating procedures and of the Bank's macroeconometric model used for the simulations.
- contribution: Step-by-step account of each link from the official rate to inflation, plus the Bank's model estimate that output responds fully after about a year and inflation after about two.
- limits: 1999; UK mortgages were then mostly floating-rate (so pass-through was faster than in fixed-rate economies like the US). Simulation band comes from two model runs with different assumptions; no probability assigned; the authors stress it is illustrative. Spot-check: the 0.2-0.35% GDP and 0.2-0.4 pp inflation figures on p.162 match the chart text on p.170. Institutional view, not independent research. Retraction search not needed for an official report; a later BoE framework has broadened tools (QE) but the interest-rate chain described is unchanged in current Fed explainer (cross-checked with Fed FAQ).
- read: full · via direct PDF

### How does the Federal Reserve affect inflation and employment?
- url: https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/money_12856.htm; text read from: same
- byline: Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System · official FAQ page · federalreserve.gov · last updated July 19, 2024 (accessed 2026-11-03)
- verdicts: C1 pass; C2 pass; C3 conditional (brief; states demand pushes up wages and costs, no detail); C8 pass ("not direct or immediate")
- use: whole page
- credibility: The US central bank describing its own primary tool, the federal funds rate.
- contribution: Current confirmation that the policy tool is an overnight interbank rate whose changes move other borrowing costs and therefore spending, hiring, wages and prices.
- limits: Short explainer; no quantities or timing.
- read: full

### Transmission Lags of Monetary Policy: A Meta-Analysis
- url: https://www.ijcb.org/journal/v9n4/transmission-lags-monetary-policy-meta-analysis; text read from: CNB Working Paper 10/2012 (https://www.cnb.cz/export/sites/cnb/en/economic-research/.galleries/research_publications/cnb_wp/cnbwp_2012_10.pdf); key numbers also checked in the IJCB published PDF
- byline: Tomáš Havránek and Marek Rusnák · peer-reviewed journal article (meta-analysis) · International Journal of Central Banking 9(4) · December 2013 (working paper October 2012)
- verdicts: C5 pass (stated as: average across 198 estimates from 67 VAR studies covering 30 economies; median of 15 months in hump-shaped responses; wide spread); C6 conditional (finds financial development associated with slower transmission; interpretation by authors); C8 pass
- use: abstract; Section 2, Figure 1 (stylized responses), Figure 2 (kernel density), Table 2 (summary statistics), Table 3 (by country)
- credibility: Economists at the Czech National Bank research department and Charles University; quantitatively pool 198 estimates from 67 published studies with inclusion criteria (normalized 1 pp shock, confidence intervals reported).
- contribution: Measures how long, across the literature, prices take to reach their strongest fall after a 1 pp rate hike: average 29 months after correcting for misspecification; raw mean 33.5 (sd 19.4); 18.2 for hump-shaped responses vs 49.1 for still-falling responses; large developed economies 25-50 months; max price-level fall about 0.9%.
- limits: Lag defined as month of strongest price-level response, which for still-falling responses is just the end of the reported horizon (inflates the mean). Studies end by 2010. VAR estimates themselves are model-dependent. Abstract figures (29 months, 0.9%, 33.5/18.2/49.1) verified identical in published IJCB version. Targeted search found no erratum or replication failure.
- read: partial (abstract, intro, Section 2 and tables read; BMA sections skimmed) · version CNB WP 10/2012, numbers cross-checked against IJCB 2013

### Five Facts about Prices: A Reevaluation of Menu Cost Models
- url: https://doi.org/10.1162/qjec.2008.123.4.1415; text read from: author copy https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/3556/fivefacts.pdf
- byline: Emi Nakamura and Jón Steinsson · peer-reviewed journal article · Quarterly Journal of Economics 123(4) · November 2008
- verdicts: C7 pass (US consumer prices 1988-2005, regular prices excluding sales); C6 conditional (shows individual prices are sticky; link to slow inflation response is standard theory, not tested here)
- use: abstract and introduction pp.1415-1416
- credibility: Uses the Bureau of Labor Statistics micro price records underlying the US CPI and PPI; authors are leading empirical macroeconomists (Nakamura later won the Clark Medal).
- contribution: Median frequency of non-sale price change 9-12% per month, implying regular prices last 8-11 months; including sales, prices change far more often (19-20% per month).
- limits: US data 1988-2005, a low-inflation era; fact 3 shows price increases become more frequent when inflation rises, so stickiness is lower in high-inflation periods. Sales make prices look more flexible than the regular price.
- read: partial (abstract, introduction, facts summary) · author copy

## Gaps
- C6: only conditional evidence that price stickiness is what slows the inflation response (well-established theory; not separately tested in the sources read).
- C5: no post-2010 direct estimate of the lag in the sources read; currentness flagged in report (e.g., more fixed-rate mortgages lengthen lags).
