# Source audit records

Claims
- C1: A central bank directly sets only a very short-term (overnight) interest rate, by choosing the rate it pays or charges banks on reserves; this passes quickly to market, loan and deposit rates.
- C2: Higher rates reduce spending by households and firms (saving vs spending, cash flow of borrowers, investment, wealth/asset prices, exchange rate).
- C3: Lower demand relative to the economy's supply capacity reduces upward pressure on wages and prices (output gap / Phillips curve).
- C4: Inflation expectations matter: real rate = nominal minus expected inflation; anchored expectations let policy work, and expectations can make inflation respond sooner.
- C5: Timing: financial conditions move within days-months; output responds most after about a year; inflation responds most after roughly one to two-plus years.
- C6: Lags arise because prices and wages are reset infrequently, many loans are fixed-rate, and spending changes ripple through inventories, orders, production, jobs and income.
- C7: The lag length is variable across countries, periods and methods (average ~29 months in the literature; 25–50 months for developed economies).
- C8: Size and cost: a 1 percentage point surprise hike lowers prices by well under a few percent and also lowers output and raises unemployment; estimates are uncertain.
- C9: Limits: effects are state-dependent and uncertain; monetary policy works mainly through demand and cannot raise long-run output.

- verdicts: C1 conditional (UK repo-based operating procedure of 1999 is historical; use for "official rate passes to market rates", not current mechanics); C2 pass; C3 pass; C4 pass (real vs nominal rate section); C5 conditional (model simulation and "empirical evidence" summary for 1990s UK; frame as illustrative); C6 pass ("Time-lags" section); C8 conditional (model magnitudes 0.2–0.4pp, illustrative only); C9 pass

### A New Measure of Monetary Shocks: Derivation and Implications
- url: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/0002828042002651 ; text read from: author copy https://eml.berkeley.edu/~dromer/papers/AER_September04.pdf
- byline: Christina D. Romer and David H. Romer · peer-reviewed journal article · American Economic Review 94(4), pp.1055–1084 · September 2004
- verdicts: C5 pass (US price level virtually unchanged for 22 months, then falls); C8 conditional (magnitude, 6% after 48 months, is at the high end and disputed; see Coibion); C7 conditional (one US estimate)
- use: introduction summary (p.1056); section on prices (pp.1073–1075), Figure 4 (p.1075)
- credibility: Primary research by economists who reconstructed the Fed's intended rate changes from FOMC records and stripped out the part predictable from the Fed's own internal forecasts, to isolate policy surprises.
- contribution: Shows that for 1969–1996 US data, a 1pp policy shock leaves producer prices roughly unchanged for about two years and then lowers them steadily; industrial production falls from month 5 and bottoms out around two years.
- limits: Price measure is PPI for finished goods; sample 1969–1996, including the volatile 1979–82 reserves-targeting period. Coibion (2012) finds the large magnitudes are driven disproportionately by two 1980 episodes and long lag specifications. Retraction/errata search: none found; critique found (Coibion).
- read: partial (abstract, introduction, price-results section and Figure 4–5 page; output section skimmed)

### Transmission Lags of Monetary Policy: A Meta-Analysis
- url: https://www.ijcb.org/journal/v9n4/transmission-lags-monetary-policy-meta-analysis ; text read from: https://www.ijcb.org/sites/default/files/journal/v9n4/ijcb-v9n4-transmission-lags-monetary-policy-meta-analysis.pdf
- byline: Tomas Havranek and Marek Rusnak · peer-reviewed meta-analysis · International Journal of Central Banking 9(4), pp.39–76 · December 2013
- verdicts: C5 pass; C7 pass; C8 conditional (0.9% average peak price fall, across heterogeneous VAR studies)
- use: abstract; introduction pp.40–41; data section pp.42–45 (Figure 1, Figure 2, Table 2); Table 3 p.46; concluding remarks
- credibility: Czech National Bank and Charles University researchers who collected 198 estimates from 67 published studies covering 30 countries and modelled why they differ.
- contribution: Average months until prices bottom out after a 1pp hike: 29 months (corrected; raw mean 33.5, SD ~19); developed economies 25–50 months, post-transition 10–20; financial development explains most cross-country variation.
- limits: Pools VAR studies of varying quality; "transmission lag" for strictly decreasing responses is set to the last reported horizon, inflating averages (hump-shaped mean 18.2 vs strictly decreasing 49.1 months). Studies span decades before 2012. Erratum/replication search: none found.
- read: partial (abstract, sections 1–3, Tables 2–3, concluding remarks; BMA details skimmed)

### Are the Effects of Monetary Policy Shocks Big or Small?
- url: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mac.4.2.1 ; text read from: NBER working paper w17034 https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w17034/w17034.pdf
- byline: Olivier Coibion · peer-reviewed journal article (working paper version read) · American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics 4(2) · April 2012 (working paper May 2011)
- verdicts: C8 conditional (supports "size of effects is uncertain; Romer–Romer magnitudes are at the high end"); C5 fail (not about timing)
- use: abstract and introduction pp.1–3
- credibility: Primary research re-estimating Romer–Romer and VAR approaches on common footing.
- contribution: Large R&R effects are disproportionately driven by two 1980 episodes and long lag choices; reconciled estimates are "medium": 100bp lowers industrial production 2–3%, raises unemployment ~0.5pp.
- limits: Working-paper version read, not the published article; focuses on real effects more than prices.
- read: partial (abstract, introduction)

## Gaps
- C5/C7: unresolved conflict in timing of peak price effect — BoE 2024 UK estimates peak after ~1 year, older UK/US and meta-analytic estimates 2+ years. Report as variability (C7) rather than one number.
- C8: only conditional magnitude evidence; ranges differ widely by method.
- Disputed premise: none; but the question's "why does it take so long" assumes a fixed lag — sources stress lags are variable.
