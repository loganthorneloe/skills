Causal inference, from the ground up.

The short answer

When app users score higher than non-users, you are comparing two different groups of students, and those groups were probably different before anyone opened the app. Students who choose a learning app tend to be more motivated, better supported, or already ahead, and those traits raise exam scores on their own. The gap you see is the app's real effect, which could be large, zero, or even negative, plus however much the users would have outscored the non-users anyway. The data alone cannot tell you how to split it.

To find out whether the app helped, you need a comparison group that is like the users in every way except the app. The most reliable way to get one is to let chance decide who gets the app, with a coin flip or a lottery. Then the two groups start out alike on average, including in traits nobody measured, and any later difference in their average scores beyond what luck would produce is the app's doing. Real trials of learning software run this way have found everything from no detectable gain to large gains, which is why the answer has to be measured rather than read off a gap between users and non-users.

A higher average is a comparison between different people

Start with what you actually observe. Suppose a school reports that students who used a learning app averaged 80 on the final exam and students who did not averaged 62. That is a difference between two averages, and it is real: those are the scores. Statisticians call this an association, meaning two things tend to show up together in the data. App use and high scores go together.

An association says what happened, not why. The 18-point gap is a fact about two groups of people. The claim "the app helped" is a different kind of statement. It is about what would have happened to the same students if things had gone otherwise, and the rest of this explanation is about the distance between those two kinds of statement.

What "the app helped" means: one student, two worlds

Take one student, Maya. Saying the app helped Maya means that her score with the app is higher than the score she would have gotten, on the same exam, had she never used it. Her causal effect is the difference between those two scores. The two scores are called her potential outcomes: one for the world where she uses the app, one for the world where she doesn't.

The difficulty is that Maya lives in only one of those worlds. If she used the app, we see her score with it, and her score without it is a "what if" that never happened. That missing score is called the counterfactual. Every student has one, and nobody can observe it. Hernán and Robins and Cunningham both build their textbooks on this point, which Cunningham calls the fundamental problem of causal inference: you can never see both outcomes for the same person, so you can never directly measure an individual's causal effect.

Cunningham makes the idea concrete with a table no real study could ever have, showing both outcomes for each of ten invented cancer patients, one under surgery and one under chemotherapy.

The effect for everyone versus the gap between groups

Since no individual effect can be seen, researchers aim for an average. The average causal effect of the app asks: if every student in the school used it, what would the average score be, and how does that compare with the average if no student used it? Both halves describe the whole school, the same people, in two "what if" worlds.

The users-versus-non-users gap asks something else. It takes the real world, where some students used the app and some didn't, and compares the average of one subset with the average of the other. The diagram below, from Hernán and Robins, draws the two comparisons side by side.

The two numbers can disagree badly. Hernán and Robins build a made-up population of 20 heart-transplant patients in which 7 of the 13 who got a transplant died (about 54%) and 3 of the 7 who didn't also died (about 43%). That looks like the transplant raises the death risk by 11 percentage points. Yet in their full "what if" table, the death risk would be 10 out of 20 if everyone got a transplant and 10 out of 20 if no one did: no effect at all. The gap appears because, in their example, the sicker patients were the ones who got transplants. They give a second example where the sign flips entirely: a drug that halves the risk of death can look like it raises the risk by half, because doctors prescribe it to the people already most at risk.

The users average 80 and the non-users average 62, so the observed gap is 18 points. The app's true effect is 2. The other 16 points come from who chose the app: even without it, the three users would have averaged 78, which is 16 points above the non-users' 62. That 16-point difference has a name, selection bias: how differently the two groups would have scored if neither had used the app. So the observed gap splits into two parts.

observed gap (18) = true effect (2) + selection bias (16)

A real school sees only the 18. The 78, the users' average in the world where they skipped the app, is a counterfactual, so nothing in the school's data reveals how the 18 divides. It could be 2 + 16, or 18 + 0, or −3 + 21 for an app that actually hurt. Students who are more motivated, have more help at home, have more time, or are already ahead are both more likely to use a study app and more likely to score well, and each of those traits feeds the selection-bias term. A trait that drives both the choice and the outcome is called a confounder.

Other ways a gap can appear without the app helping

Self-selection on motivation or preparation is the most common trap, but three other comparisons that look like evidence fail for related reasons.

Reverse causation

The arrow can point the other way. Students who are doing well may enjoy a study app and keep using it, while struggling students give up on it. Then high scores lead to app use, not the reverse, and the gap appears even if the app teaches nothing.

Before and after

Comparing users' scores this spring with their scores last fall mixes the app's effect with everything else that happened in between: a year of teaching, growing older, practice with the test. The World Bank handbook calls this kind of estimate a "counterfeit" counterfactual, because last year's score is almost never a good stand-in for what would have happened this year without the program.

More use, higher scores

Finding that students who used the app more hours scored higher is still a comparison between students who chose different amounts of use. The most motivated students log the most hours. The federal evaluators who ran the randomized trial described below found larger effects in fourth-grade classrooms that used the software more, and warned that the pattern had no causal interpretation.

That last point is worth holding onto, because it applies even inside a well-run experiment. Randomization makes the comparison between the group assigned the app and the group not assigned it fair. It does nothing to make a comparison between heavy and light users fair, because students still chose how much to use it. The evaluators put it directly:

"effects were larger when teachers reported higher levels of product use. As noted above, these relationships do not have a causal interpretation."

Dynarski and colleagues, on fourth-grade reading, IES executive summary, NCEE 2007-4006, p. xii↗ (public domain)

Why "controlling for" differences doesn't fix it

A natural response is to compare like with like. Instead of all users against all non-users, compare users and non-users who had the same grades last year, the same family income, the same school. Statistical tools for this, such as regression (which estimates the gap while holding measured traits fixed) and matching (which pairs each user with a similar non-user), are what people mean by "controlling for" a variable.

These tools remove differences you measured. They cannot remove differences you did not measure, and motivation, curiosity, or a parent who checks homework are rarely in the data. Hernán and Robins add the harder point: even if you did happen to measure every trait that matters, the data could not confirm it, because the evidence needed to check is the counterfactual score you never observe. Adjusted comparisons therefore rest on an assumption that cannot be verified from the data.

The clearest test of how much this matters came from a job-training program, not an app, but the logic is identical. The National Supported Work Demonstration had randomly assigned applicants to training or no training, so its true effect on 1978 earnings was known: about $886. LaLonde threw away the randomized comparison group and substituted people drawn from national surveys, then applied the adjustments economists of the time relied on, controlling for age, schooling, dropout status, and race.

The fix: let chance decide who gets the app

Everything so far comes down to one problem: the users' no-app average is missing, and the non-users are a poor stand-in for it because they differ from users in ways that matter. Random assignment solves this by taking the choice away from students. Flip a coin for each student; heads get the app, tails don't. Motivated and unmotivated students, students with and without help at home, students who would love the app and students who would ignore it, all land on each side in about equal shares, because a coin knows nothing about any of them. That includes traits nobody thought to measure.

Once the two groups are alike on average, the tails group's average is a fair stand-in for what the heads group would have scored without the app. Selection bias is zero on average, so the difference between the groups' average scores estimates the app's average causal effect. Hernán and Robins sum it up in one line: "In ideal randomized experiments, association is causation."

"On average" is doing real work in that sentence. A single coin-flip split can be unlucky; with six students, all three motivated ones might land on heads. Bigger groups make large chance imbalances rare. The simulator below lets you watch both effects. Two things show up when you play with it. The self-chosen gap stays near the true effect plus about 16 or 17 no matter how many students there are, because more students do not make the comparison any fairer. The coin-flip gaps scatter around the true effect, and the scatter shrinks as the school grows: with 20 students a single flip can miss by 8 points or more, with 1,000 it rarely misses by more than 1 or 2. Setting the true effect to 0 shows that the self-chosen comparison still reports a large "benefit" for an app that does nothing.

That scatter is why real trials do two more things. First, they check that randomization produced similar groups before the app was used, which is called checking balance. Second, they report how much of the final gap could be chance. A p-value is one way of doing that: it asks how often a gap at least this large would appear by luck alone if the app did nothing, and a small p-value (conventionally below 0.05) means luck alone would rarely produce it. A p-value says nothing about whether a comparison was fair to begin with. In the World Bank handbook's fictional insurance example, the self-selected comparison is overwhelmingly "significant" and still off by more than four dollars from the randomized answer.

What real trials of learning software found

Randomized trials of learning software exist, and they show why the answer has to be measured rather than assumed. Researchers report the size of a gain in standard deviations (SD), a unit for how spread out scores are. On a typical bell-shaped test, one SD is roughly the distance from the 50th percentile to the 84th, so a gain of 0.1 SD is small and a gain of 0.4 SD is large. The review by Escueta and colleagues offers a rule of thumb for education: under 0.10 SD is small, 0.10 to 0.25 encouraging, 0.25 to 0.40 large, and above 0.40 very large.

The federal study was large. In the 2004–05 school year, about 9,400 students in 132 schools across 33 districts were tested in the fall and spring, after their teachers were randomly assigned to use one of 16 reading or math products or to teach as usual. After one year, classrooms using the software did not score measurably higher.

That result has limits that matter. The products were volunteered by their makers, results were pooled across groups of products, teachers were using them for the first time, and the software filled only about a tenth of instructional time. A second-year follow-up found that one of ten individual products, a fourth-grade reading program, had a statistically significant positive effect. The study shows that these products, used this way, did not detectably raise scores; it does not show that learning software cannot work.

The Delhi study shows the opposite outcome from the same method. More middle-school students applied for an after-school program built around adaptive software, called Mindspark, than there were seats, so a lottery chose 314 of 619 applicants to get free access. Lottery winners and losers were then tested before and after about four and a half months.

A gain of 0.37 SD would move a student from the middle of the class to roughly the 64th percentile, which counts as large by the rule of thumb above. It also has limits. The program combined software with a teaching assistant and extra hours, so the software's separate contribution was not isolated. The math gains showed up mainly on questions below the students' grade level and did not appear on their schools' own exams. And the study covers 619 students who applied, in three centers in one city.

Across the wider evidence, Escueta and colleagues counted 29 randomized trials of computer-assisted learning in wealthy countries: 20 found gains, 15 of those in math, 8 found no effect, and 1 found a negative effect. They restricted themselves to such trials because, in their assessment, research on education technology is crowded with the kind of observational comparison this explanation warns about. Their tally counts studies rather than averaging effect sizes, so it says that gains are common, not how big they typically are. None of these trials tested a phone app that students use on their own, so the evidence speaks to classroom and after-school software rather than to every learning app.

What a real trial actually measures

Real experiments are messier than coin flips in a simulator, and each wrinkle changes what the final number means.

  • Not everyone offered the app uses it. In Delhi, lottery winners attended only 58% of the time on average. Comparing everyone offered with everyone not offered, called the intent-to-treat comparison, keeps the fairness that randomization bought, but it measures the effect of offering the app. The researchers used the lottery in an extra step to estimate the effect of actually attending, about 0.6 SD in math for 90 days of attendance, an estimate that rests on further assumptions about how gains grow with attendance.
  • Some students miss the final test. In Delhi, 84% of winners and 88% of losers were retested. If the students who drop out differ between groups, the groups are no longer alike, so the researchers checked how much the missing students could change the result.
  • Effects can spill over. If students assigned the app share it with friends in the comparison group, or teachers change how they teach everyone, the comparison group no longer shows what happens without the app.
  • Results belong to a product, a place, a way of using it, and a test. The federal evaluators wrote that their findings did not support conclusions about technology's effectiveness beyond the study's context. A result for one app in one setting is a strong answer to a narrow question.

When you cannot flip a coin

Sometimes randomizing is impossible, unethical, or too late; the app is already out and people chose it. Researchers then look for a situation that comes close to a coin flip, and each such design trades the coin for an assumption that cannot be fully checked.

  • Natural experiments and lotteries use some event that assigned access as if at random, such as an oversubscribed program that filled seats by lottery. The assumption is that the event really was unrelated to students' prospects.
  • Regression discontinuity applies when a cutoff decides access, for example, students scoring below 60 on a placement test get the app. Students at 59 and 61 are nearly identical, so comparing those just below and just above the cutoff approximates a fair comparison. The assumption is that nothing else changes at the cutoff, and the answer applies only to students near it.
  • Difference-in-differences compares how much users' scores changed over time with how much non-users' scores changed. The assumption is that, without the app, both groups would have changed by the same amount.
  • Matching and regression compare users with non-users who look similar on measured traits. As LaLonde showed, the assumption that no unmeasured trait differs is the one most likely to fail.

What this means when you read a claim

When someone says users of a learning app score higher, the first question is "compared with whom, and how were they chosen?" If the users chose themselves, the gap mixes the app's effect with every difference between the kinds of students who pick it up and those who don't, and nothing in the gap says how much is which. Adjusting for measured traits can shrink that mix but cannot certify that it is gone, and in the one setting where adjustment was graded against a known answer, it was often far off.

A comparison in which chance chose who got the app is the strongest evidence available, and it is still evidence about a specific product, used a specific way, by specific students, on a specific test, over a specific time. The two trials here, one finding nothing detectable and one finding a large gain, both used that method correctly, and they disagree because the products, settings, and amounts of use differed. The honest conclusion about any particular app is the one its own fair comparison supports, and where no such comparison exists, the honest conclusion is that nobody knows yet. A gap between users and non-users does not show that the app failed either; the real effect could be hidden inside it at any size.
