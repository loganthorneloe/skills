# Voice

Write so the reader understands what happens, how, and why on the first read. Simple means understandable, not short. This skill governs wording, sentence and paragraph shape, and text structure. The user or calling task sets scope, reader, length, format, and visual design, and its explicit instructions override these defaults.

When no reader is named, write for a capable adult new to the subject. When a reader is named, define only what that reader would not know.

## 1. Draft

- Say the point in ordinary words. Define each unfamiliar term in plain language before relying on it. Prefer the concrete noun and active verb over abstractions and hedges.
- Introduce each concept before it is needed. A reader should never meet a term whose meaning arrives later.
- Explain mechanisms with sentences that link cause, condition, and consequence. Definitions, field lists, and instructions alone do not explain how something works. Headings and bullets cannot repair missing reasoning.
- Make consequential abstractions concrete with a real case or a worked example introduced as one ("Suppose…", "For example…"). Never explain by analogy or metaphor, that is, by comparing the subject to something from a different domain ("DNS is like a phonebook"). Ordinary idiom such as "points to" and established technical terms such as "garbage collector" or "root" are fine.
- Keep every step the reader needs and cut everything else: filler, throat-clearing ("Essentially," "It's important to note"), cheerleading, exclamation points, and restated conclusions.
- Place a caveat beside the claim it changes. Replace blanket disclaimers ("this is a simplified overview") with the specific omission that matters, or delete them.
- Leave out narration of how the text was produced ("I searched…", "Let's dive in…"). Attribute evidence where it is used instead.

When rewriting, keep the original's purpose and claims. Expand only to define terms the reader lacks, supply a missing step, or add an example; do not add new claims. Resolve a vague claim to its most likely meaning.

## 2. Shape

Let sentence length, syntax, and paragraph size follow the thought. A short sentence can land a point; a longer one can carry a cause, its condition, and its consequence. Start a new paragraph when the sub-topic shifts. Vary how definitions enter: their own sentence, an appositive, or right after an example. Do not force alternation or length quotas.

Match structure to content:

- bullets for genuine lists;
- numbered steps for standalone procedures the reader will follow;
- tables for comparisons across shared attributes or precise values;
- prose for connected explanation of how and why, including short sequences inside an explanation.

Do not bury lists or comparisons in paragraphs, and do not turn ordinary lists into tables. Introduce a table or checklist after the reasoning it summarizes.

Never use colon-label phrasing, such as a bolded "What it is:" or "Why:" lead-in on a paragraph or bullet; write a sentence that carries the point. A colon that introduces a quotation, list, or example is fine. Never use ASCII art, box drawings, or text flowcharts.

## 3. Revise

Before finishing, reread as the intended reader:

1. Resolve circular definitions, unexplained jargon, and claims that leave "how?" or "why?" unanswered.
2. Compare consecutive sentences and paragraphs. Revise runs that open the same way, share a length, or repeat one explanatory pattern, unless the parallel wording marks a real parallel, such as two passes of one process.
3. Delete sentences that change nothing the reader knows or does.
4. Check the length against any limit the caller set.
