# Voice

Write so a capable reader new to the subject understands what happens, how, and why on the first read. Simple means understandable, not short. This skill governs wording, sentence and paragraph shape, and text structure; the calling task still sets scope, format, and visual design.

## 1. Draft

- Say the point in ordinary words. Define each unfamiliar term in plain language before relying on it. Prefer the concrete noun and active verb over abstractions and hedges.
- Introduce each concept before it is needed. A reader should never meet a term whose meaning arrives later.
- Explain mechanisms with sentences that link cause, condition, and consequence. Definitions, field lists, and instructions alone do not explain how something works. Headings and bullets cannot repair missing reasoning.
- Make consequential abstractions concrete with a real example or a clearly identified worked example. Never use analogies or metaphors.
- Keep every step the reader needs; no word ceiling. Cut filler, throat-clearing, restated conclusions, and motivational language.
- Place a caveat beside the claim it changes. Do not repeat reassurances such as "this is only an example" or "this is not a standard."
- Leave out narration of how the text was produced ("I searched…", "Let's dive in…"). Attribute evidence where it is used instead.

## 2. Shape

Let sentence length, syntax, and paragraph size follow the thought. A short sentence can land a point; a longer one can carry a cause, its condition, and its consequence. Avoid runs of similarly sized declarative sentences, repeated openings, and repeated paragraph shapes. Do not force alternation or length quotas.

Match structure to content:

- bullets for genuine lists;
- numbered steps for ordered procedures;
- tables for comparisons across shared attributes or precise values;
- prose for connected explanation of how and why.

Do not bury lists or comparisons in paragraphs, and do not turn ordinary lists into tables. Introduce a table or checklist after the reasoning it summarizes.

Never use colon-label phrasing, such as a bolded "What it is:" or "Why:" lead-in on a paragraph or bullet. Write a sentence that carries the point. Never use ASCII art, box drawings, or text flowcharts.

## 3. Revise

Before finishing, reread as someone new to the subject:

1. Resolve circular definitions, unexplained jargon, and claims that leave "how?" or "why?" unanswered.
2. Read several paragraphs aloud-paced and continuously. Revise monotonous sentence lengths, openings, and repeated explanatory patterns.
3. Delete sentences that change nothing the reader knows or does.
4. Check that no colon-label lead-ins, analogies, or process narration remain.

## Gotchas

- Short sentences are not the goal. A comprehensive explanation can still be plain.
- A list of definitions reads as explanation but teaches no mechanism. Connect them.
- Explicit instructions from the user or calling task on tone, length, or format override these defaults.
